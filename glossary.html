AI on devices

AI model

AI on devices

An AI model is a trained program that spots patterns and makes guesses. It learns from many examples. A model might name objects in a photo or words in speech.

Why tinkerers care: The size of a model decides which boards can run it.

Related terms: Inference, Training, TinyML, Edge AI, Quantization

Cloud

AI on devices

The cloud means computers in big data centers that you reach over the internet. Many gadgets send their data there. The company running the servers controls them.

Why tinkerers care: Cloud-based gear can stop working once its service shuts down.

Related terms: Edge AI, Home Assistant, Wi-Fi

Appears on 17 entries.

Computer vision

AI on devices

Computer vision is AI that understands images and video. It can spot objects, read text, or track faces. Object detection draws boxes around things it finds.

Why tinkerers care: Vision boards let you build smart cameras that count birds or sort parts.

Related terms: Camera module, NPU, AI model, Inference

Edge AI

AI on devices

Edge AI means running AI on the device itself. The data stays local and skips the cloud. Answers come back faster too.

Why tinkerers care: Edge AI keeps camera and voice data private and works with no internet.

Related terms: TinyML, NPU, Inference, Cloud

Appears on 9 entries.

Edge Impulse

AI on devices

Edge Impulse is a web platform for building TinyML models. You collect data, train a model, and export it to a board. It has a free plan for makers.

Why tinkerers care: Edge Impulse lets beginners make a working AI gadget with little coding.

Related terms: TinyML, Training, LiteRT

Inference

AI on devices

Inference is running a trained AI model on new data. The model looks at an input and gives an answer. This is the step a board does in the field.

Why tinkerers care: Inference speed tells you how many camera frames or voice commands a board can handle each second.

Related terms: AI model, Training, NPU, Edge AI

Appears on 1 entry.

Keyword spotting

AI on devices

Keyword spotting is AI that listens for one short word or phrase. It runs all the time using little power. Smart speakers use it to wake up.

Why tinkerers care: Keyword spotting lets a voice gadget react with no cloud connection.

Related terms: TinyML, MEMS microphone, Edge AI

Appears on 3 entries.

LiteRT

AI on devices

LiteRT is Google's tool for running AI models on small devices. It used to be called TensorFlow Lite. A micro version runs on microcontrollers.

Why tinkerers care: Support for LiteRT or ONNX means many ready models will run on a board.

Related terms: TinyML, AI model, Edge Impulse

On-device language model

AI on devices

An on-device language model is a chatbot-style AI that runs on local hardware. Small versions can run on strong SBCs and AI boards. They answer more slowly than cloud chatbots.

Why tinkerers care: A local language model keeps chats private and works offline.

Related terms: Edge AI, NPU, RAM, Quantization

Appears on 4 entries.

Quantization

AI on devices

Quantization shrinks an AI model by storing its numbers with fewer bits. The model gets smaller and faster. It may lose a little accuracy.

Why tinkerers care: Quantization is often the trick that makes a model fit on a small board.

Related terms: AI model, TinyML, NPU

TinyML

AI on devices

TinyML is machine learning on microcontrollers. The models are small enough to fit in a few hundred kilobytes. They can run for months on a battery.

Why tinkerers care: TinyML lets a cheap board hear a wake word or sense a gesture.

Related terms: Edge AI, AI model, LiteRT, Keyword spotting, Microcontroller

Training

AI on devices

Training is how an AI model learns from examples. The model adjusts itself bit by bit to make fewer mistakes. It usually happens on a strong computer.

Why tinkerers care: You can train a small model on a laptop and then run it on a tiny board.

Related terms: AI model, Inference, Edge Impulse

Boards and brains

Arduino

Boards and brains

Arduino is an Italian company and a family of open hardware boards. It also makes free tools for coding them. The Arduino Uno is the classic starter board.

Why tinkerers care: Arduino has one of the biggest collections of guides, code, and add-ons.

Related terms: Arduino IDE, Sketch, Shield, Microcontroller, Open hardware

Appears on 115 entries.

Board

Boards and brains

A board is a flat card covered in chips, parts, and the wires between them. Most gear in this guide is built on one. People often say board to mean the whole device.

Why tinkerers care: Knowing the kind of board tells you how much setup and power it needs.

Related terms: Printed circuit board, Microcontroller, Single-board computer, Development board

Breakout board

Boards and brains

A breakout board puts one tiny part on a small board with easy pins. The part is often a sensor or a chip too small to solder by hand. You wire the breakout to your main board.

Why tinkerers care: Breakouts let beginners use tiny modern parts on a breadboard.

Related terms: Breadboard, Pin header, Qwiic, STEMMA QT, Sensor

Appears on 9 entries.

Carrier board

Boards and brains

A carrier board holds a compute module and adds ports. It supplies power, USB, network jacks, and other plugs. Many companies sell carrier boards for the same module.

Why tinkerers care: The carrier board sets the cameras, drives, and cables you can hook up.

Related terms: Compute module, PCIe, HDMI

Appears on 19 entries.

Compute module

Boards and brains

A compute module is the brain of a computer on a small card. It has no big ports of its own. It snaps into a carrier board that adds the connectors.

Why tinkerers care: Compute modules let you build a custom product around a well-supported computer.

Related terms: Carrier board, Single-board computer, Raspberry Pi, Module

Appears on 2 entries.

Development board

Boards and brains

A development board is a chip mounted on a friendly board for testing ideas. It adds a USB port, pin headers, and often a few buttons and lights. A dev kit may also include cables and sample parts.

Why tinkerers care: Dev boards let you try a chip with only a USB cable and a computer.

Related terms: Microcontroller, Breakout board, Pin header, USB-C

Appears on 11 entries.

ESP32

Boards and brains

The ESP32 is a family of low-cost microcontroller chips from Espressif. Most have Wi-Fi and Bluetooth built in. Hundreds of boards from many makers use them.

Why tinkerers care: An ESP32 gives you a wireless gadget for the price of a sandwich.

Related terms: Microcontroller, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth Low Energy, ESP-IDF, ESPHome

Appears on 46 entries.

Feather

Boards and brains

Feather is a family of small boards started by Adafruit. All Feathers share one shape and pin layout. Many have a built-in battery charger.

Why tinkerers care: You can swap one Feather for another and keep the same add-ons.

Related terms: FeatherWing, Form factor, LiPo battery

Appears on 16 entries.

FeatherWing

Boards and brains

A FeatherWing is an add-on board shaped for the Feather family. It stacks above or below a Feather board. Wings add displays, radios, motor drivers, and more.

Why tinkerers care: Wings let you mix and match features across any Feather board.

Related terms: Feather, Shield, HAT

Form factor

Boards and brains

Form factor means the size, shape, and pin layout of a board. Boards with the same form factor fit the same add-ons and cases. Uno, Feather, and Raspberry Pi are common form factors.

Why tinkerers care: A popular form factor opens up a large shelf of add-ons and cases.

Related terms: Shield, HAT, Feather

Appears on 2 entries.

HAT

Boards and brains

A HAT is an add-on board that sits on top of a Raspberry Pi. It plugs into the 40-pin header. The name stands for Hardware Attached on Top.

Why tinkerers care: HATs add AI chips, sound, displays, or motor control to a Pi in seconds.

Related terms: Raspberry Pi, Shield, Pin header, FeatherWing

Appears on 6 entries.

micro:bit

Boards and brains

The micro:bit is a pocket-sized learning board from the Micro:bit Educational Foundation. It has an LED grid, buttons, sensors, and Bluetooth. Kids can code it with blocks in a web browser.

Why tinkerers care: The micro:bit is one of the gentlest first steps into electronics for any age.

Related terms: MakeCode, Block coding, Microcontroller, Newcomer friendly

Appears on 8 entries.

Microcontroller

Boards and brains

A microcontroller is a tiny computer on a single chip. It runs one program over and over. It can read sensors and switch lights or motors on and off.

Why tinkerers care: Microcontrollers are cheap, sip power, and start in a blink. They sit at the heart of most gadgets.

Related terms: Single-board computer, Firmware, GPIO, Arm Cortex-M, ESP32, Deep sleep

Appears on 36 entries.

Module

Boards and brains

A module is a small ready-made board that does one job. It might hold a chip, a radio, and an antenna in a shielded can. Makers solder or plug modules onto bigger boards.

Why tinkerers care: Modules come pre-tested and often pre-certified, which saves a lot of design work.

Related terms: Breakout board, Compute module, Development board, Antenna

Appears on 63 entries.

Raspberry Pi

Boards and brains

Raspberry Pi is a line of low-cost single-board computers from a UK charity and company. The boards run Linux. The same group also makes the Pico microcontroller boards.

Why tinkerers care: The Pi has a huge community. Answers to most problems are a quick search away.

Related terms: Single-board computer, Raspberry Pi Pico, HAT, Raspberry Pi OS, Compute module

Appears on 58 entries.

Raspberry Pi Pico

Boards and brains

The Raspberry Pi Pico is a small microcontroller board. It uses the RP2040 or RP2350 chip made by Raspberry Pi. The W versions add Wi-Fi and Bluetooth.

Why tinkerers care: The Pico costs only a few dollars and runs MicroPython with very little setup.

Related terms: Microcontroller, Raspberry Pi, MicroPython, Programmable I/O, UF2

Appears on 20 entries.

Shield

Boards and brains

A shield is an add-on board that stacks on top of an Arduino-shaped board. It plugs straight into the pin headers. Shields add things like motor drivers, displays, or radios.

Why tinkerers care: Shields add a feature with no wiring at all.

Related terms: HAT, FeatherWing, Arduino, Form factor

Appears on 13 entries.

Single-board computer

Boards and brains

A single-board computer is a full computer on one small board. It runs an operating system such as Linux. You can plug in a screen, keyboard, and mouse.

Why tinkerers care: An SBC can run desktop apps, web servers, and AI models too big for a microcontroller.

Related terms: Microcontroller, Raspberry Pi, Linux, System on a chip, microSD card

Appears on 2 entries.

System on a chip

Boards and brains

A system on a chip puts many parts of a computer into one chip. It often holds the processor, graphics, memory control, and radios. Phones and single-board computers use them.

Why tinkerers care: The SoC sets the speed, power use, and software support of the whole board.

Related terms: CPU, GPU, NPU, Single-board computer, Microcontroller

Appears on 1 entry.

Cameras, audio, and wearables

Amplifier

Cameras, audio, and wearables

An amplifier makes a weak sound signal strong enough to drive a speaker. Small class D amplifiers are efficient and cool. Some take digital sound over I2S.

Why tinkerers care: A board needs an amplifier to play sound loud enough to hear across a room.

Related terms: Audio codec, I2S, DAC

Appears on 45 entries.

Audio codec

Cameras, audio, and wearables

An audio codec is a chip that turns sound into data and back again. It holds an ADC for input and a DAC for output. Audio add-on boards are built around one.

Why tinkerers care: A good codec makes the difference between buzzy and clear sound.

Related terms: DAC, ADC, I2S, Amplifier

Appears on 4 entries.

Camera module

Cameras, audio, and wearables

A camera module is a small board with an image sensor and a lens. It plugs into a board through a ribbon cable or connector. Some have their own processor built in.

Why tinkerers care: A camera module turns a board into a security cam, a wildlife cam, or a vision AI demo.

Related terms: MIPI CSI, Computer vision, Resolution

Appears on 123 entries.

MEMS microphone

Cameras, audio, and wearables

A MEMS microphone is a tiny microphone made like a computer chip. Phones and smart speakers use them. Many send digital sound over I2S or PDM.

Why tinkerers care: A digital MEMS mic gives a board clean sound for voice commands and recording.

Related terms: I2S, Keyword spotting, Audio codec

Appears on 42 entries.

Resolution

Cameras, audio, and wearables

Resolution is how many dots make up an image or screen. It is often given in megapixels or as width by height. More dots show finer detail.

Why tinkerers care: Higher resolution needs more memory and processing, which matters on small boards.

Related terms: Camera module, Display, Computer vision

Appears on 24 entries.

Wearable

Cameras, audio, and wearables

A wearable is a gadget you wear on your body or clothes. It might be a watch, a badge, or glowing fabric. Wearable boards are small, light, and run on batteries.

Why tinkerers care: Wearable boards bring electronics into fashion, cosplay, and health tracking.

Related terms: Bluetooth Low Energy, LiPo battery, NeoPixel, IMU

Chips and specs

Actuator

Chips and specs

An actuator is a part that makes something happen in the world. Motors, speakers, and relays are actuators. A board tells it what to do.

Why tinkerers care: Actuators let a project move, buzz, or switch other gear on.

Related terms: Sensor, Servo motor, Motor driver

ADC

Chips and specs

An ADC turns a smooth analog signal into a number. It lets a board measure things like light level, sound, or battery voltage. More bits give finer readings.

Why tinkerers care: A good ADC matters for projects that measure the real world with care.

Related terms: Analog, DAC, Sensor, GPIO

Appears on 30 entries.

Analog

Chips and specs

An analog signal can take any value in a range. A dimmer knob or a temperature reading changes smoothly. Computers must convert analog signals into numbers.

Why tinkerers care: Many simple sensors give an analog output that needs an ADC pin.

Related terms: Digital, ADC, DAC

Appears on 39 entries.

Arm Cortex-A

Chips and specs

Cortex-A is a family of Arm processor designs for full computers. Phones and most single-board computers use them. They run operating systems like Linux and Android.

Why tinkerers care: The Cortex-A model on an SBC gives a quick hint of its speed.

Related terms: Arm Cortex-M, Single-board computer, System on a chip

Appears on 2 entries.

Arm Cortex-M

Chips and specs

Cortex-M is a family of Arm processor designs for microcontrollers. Chip makers license the design from Arm. Higher numbers usually mean more speed and features.

Why tinkerers care: Cortex-M is the most common brain in hobby microcontroller boards. Tools and help are easy to find.

Related terms: Microcontroller, Arm Cortex-A, RISC-V

Appears on 8 entries.

Clock speed

Chips and specs

Clock speed is how many steps a processor ticks through each second. It is measured in megahertz or gigahertz. One megahertz means one million ticks per second.

Why tinkerers care: Clock speed gives a rough sense of power, though chip design matters just as much.

Related terms: CPU, Core, Capacity score

Appears on 183 entries.

Core

Chips and specs

A core is one worker inside a processor. Each core can run its own list of steps. A quad-core chip has four of them.

Why tinkerers care: More cores let a board handle several jobs at once, such as reading a sensor and running Wi-Fi.

Related terms: CPU, Clock speed, Capacity score

Appears on 144 entries.

CPU

Chips and specs

The CPU is the main chip part that runs program steps. It does math, makes choices, and moves data around. Its speed shapes how fast the device feels.

Why tinkerers care: A faster CPU runs bigger programs, but it also uses more power and makes more heat.

Related terms: Core, Clock speed, System on a chip, GPU, NPU

Appears on 75 entries.

DAC

Chips and specs

A DAC turns numbers into a smooth analog signal. Sound output is the most common use. A DAC lets a board make real voltage levels between off and full.

Why tinkerers care: A board with a DAC can play audio or drive analog gear with fewer extra parts.

Related terms: ADC, Analog, Audio codec, I2S

Appears on 3 entries.

Digital

Chips and specs

A digital signal has only two states, on or off. Computers read these as 1 and 0. Buttons and most data links are digital.

Why tinkerers care: Digital signals resist noise, which makes them easy to work with.

Related terms: Analog, GPIO, Logic level

Appears on 25 entries.

Display

Chips and specs

A display is a screen for text, shapes, or pictures. Small boards often use color LCD screens. Bigger ones connect over HDMI or MIPI DSI.

Why tinkerers care: A built-in screen lets a project show results with no computer attached.

Related terms: OLED, E-paper, MIPI DSI, HDMI

Appears on 147 entries.

E-paper

Chips and specs

E-paper is a screen that looks like printed paper. It keeps its image with no power at all. It updates slowly.

Why tinkerers care: E-paper suits battery gadgets with screens changing only now and then.

Related terms: Display, OLED, Deep sleep

Appears on 6 entries.

eMMC

Chips and specs

eMMC is flash storage soldered right onto a board. It works like a built-in memory card. It is faster and tougher than most microSD cards.

Why tinkerers care: Boards with eMMC boot faster and hold up better in long-running projects.

Related terms: Flash memory, microSD card, Single-board computer

Appears on 3 entries.

Flash memory

Chips and specs

Flash memory is storage that keeps its data with the power off. Microcontrollers keep their program in flash. It is slower to write than RAM.

Why tinkerers care: Flash size limits how much code, sound, or settings a board can store.

Related terms: RAM, eMMC, Flashing, Firmware

Appears on 25 entries.

FPGA

Chips and specs

An FPGA is a chip whose wiring you can rearrange with code. You describe a circuit and the chip becomes that circuit. You can change it again later.

Why tinkerers care: FPGAs let you build custom hardware for very fast or very exact jobs.

Related terms: LUT, Hardware description language, Open silicon, Toolchain

Appears on 23 entries.

GPIO

Chips and specs

GPIO pins are metal pins you can control from code. Each one can read a signal or send one out. They connect your board to buttons, lights, and sensors.

Why tinkerers care: More GPIO pins means more things you can wire to one board.

Related terms: Pin, Pin header, ADC, PWM, Logic level

Appears on 7 entries.

GPU

Chips and specs

A GPU is a chip part built to do many small math jobs at once. It first drew graphics for games. Today it also speeds up AI work.

Why tinkerers care: A strong GPU lets a board show smooth video or run larger AI models.

Related terms: CPU, NPU, System on a chip, Inference

Appears on 14 entries.

Hardware description language

Chips and specs

A hardware description language is code that describes a circuit. Verilog and VHDL are the best known. Tools turn this code into a setup for an FPGA or a chip design.

Why tinkerers care: Learning an HDL is the main step between blinking LEDs and building your own chip logic.

Related terms: FPGA, LUT, Toolchain

Appears on 10 entries.

LED

Chips and specs

An LED is a small light that turns on with very little power. It only lets power flow one way. Most boards have at least one LED for status.

Why tinkerers care: Blinking an LED is the classic first program on any new board.

Related terms: NeoPixel, PWM, GPIO

Appears on 79 entries.

LUT

Chips and specs

A LUT is a tiny building block inside an FPGA. Each one can act as a small logic gate. FPGA size is often given in thousands of LUTs.

Why tinkerers care: More LUTs means room for bigger circuits, such as a whole soft processor.

Related terms: FPGA, Hardware description language

Appears on 14 entries.

microSD card

Chips and specs

A microSD card is a tiny removable memory card. Many single-board computers boot their whole operating system from one. Microcontroller boards use them to log data.

Why tinkerers care: A good microSD card is often a hidden extra cost for an SBC.

Related terms: eMMC, Single-board computer, Raspberry Pi OS

Appears on 75 entries.

NeoPixel

Chips and specs

A NeoPixel is a color LED with its own tiny control chip. You can chain many on one data wire. Code sets the color of each one.

Why tinkerers care: NeoPixels make glowing wearables and light shows easy with just three wires.

Related terms: LED, Programmable I/O, Wearable

Appears on 6 entries.

NPU

Chips and specs

An NPU is a chip part made just for running AI models. It does AI math faster and with less power than a CPU. Some boards have one built in and some add it on.

Why tinkerers care: An NPU lets a small board spot objects in live camera video.

Related terms: TOPS, Edge AI, Inference, GPU, AI model

Appears on 11 entries.

OLED

Chips and specs

An OLED is a screen where each dot makes its own light. It has deep blacks and sharp contrast. Tiny OLED screens are common on maker boards.

Why tinkerers care: A small OLED is a cheap way to show readings on a gadget.

Related terms: Display, E-paper, I2C

Appears on 2 entries.

Open silicon

Chips and specs

Open silicon is a chip whose inner design files are public. Anyone can study, copy, or change the design. Most chips keep these files secret.

Why tinkerers care: Open silicon means you can trust and inspect the hardware all the way down.

Related terms: RISC-V, FPGA, Open hardware

Pin

Chips and specs

A pin is a metal point where a wire or part connects to a board. A pinout is a map that shows what each pin does. Some pins carry power and others carry signals.

Why tinkerers care: Reading the pinout first keeps you from wiring a part to the wrong spot.

Related terms: GPIO, Pin header, Ground

Programmable I/O

Chips and specs

Programmable I/O is a set of tiny helper processors inside the RP2040 and RP2350 chips. They handle pin timing on their own. Makers use them to invent new ways to talk to parts.

Why tinkerers care: PIO lets a cheap Pico drive odd displays, LED strips, and old game controllers.

Related terms: Raspberry Pi Pico, GPIO, NeoPixel

Appears on 2 entries.

PSRAM

Chips and specs

PSRAM is extra RAM added next to a microcontroller. It is slower than SRAM but much bigger. Boards often list it in megabytes.

Why tinkerers care: PSRAM lets a small board hold camera images, screen buffers, or tiny AI models.

Related terms: SRAM, RAM, ESP32, TinyML

Appears on 10 entries.

PWM

Chips and specs

PWM flips a pin on and off very fast. The share of on time sets the average power. It can dim an LED or set a motor speed.

Why tinkerers care: PWM pins drive servos, fade lights, and control motors from plain digital pins.

Related terms: GPIO, Servo motor, LED, Motor driver

RAM

Chips and specs

RAM is the short-term memory a chip uses while it runs. It holds the data a program is working on right now. It empties out once the power goes off.

Why tinkerers care: RAM limits how big a program, image, or AI model the board can handle at once.

Related terms: SRAM, PSRAM, Flash memory, Capacity score

Appears on 89 entries.

RISC-V

Chips and specs

RISC-V is a free and open set of rules for how a processor works. Anyone can design a RISC-V chip without paying fees. It is now found in many microcontrollers and some SBCs.

Why tinkerers care: RISC-V chips bring openness down to the processor itself.

Related terms: Arm Cortex-M, Open silicon, CPU, Open hardware

Appears on 34 entries.

Sensor

Chips and specs

A sensor is a part that measures something in the world. It might sense heat, light, motion, sound, or gas. It sends the reading to a board as a signal.

Why tinkerers care: Sensors are how a project notices what is going on around it.

Related terms: Actuator, ADC, I2C, IMU, Breakout board

Appears on 192 entries.

SRAM

Chips and specs

SRAM is a fast kind of RAM built right into a microcontroller chip. It is very quick but small. Many boards have only a few hundred kilobytes.

Why tinkerers care: SRAM size often decides whether your code and data fit on a microcontroller.

Related terms: RAM, PSRAM, Microcontroller

TOPS

Chips and specs

TOPS stands for trillion operations per second. It is a rough measure of AI speed. Makers usually quote it for their NPU.

Why tinkerers care: TOPS helps compare AI boards, though real speed also depends on the software.

Related terms: NPU, Edge AI, Capacity score

Connections and buses

Breadboard

Connections and buses

A breadboard is a plastic block full of small holes linked in rows. You push parts and wires into it to build a circuit. Nothing needs solder.

Why tinkerers care: Breadboards let you test an idea and change it in minutes.

Related terms: Jumper wire, Pin header, Breakout board

Appears on 19 entries.

Bus

Connections and buses

A bus is a shared path that carries data between parts. It has rules for who talks and when. I2C, SPI, and USB are all buses.

Why tinkerers care: Matching the bus of a sensor to your board is a key wiring step.

Related terms: I2C, SPI, UART, CAN bus

Appears on 5 entries.

CAN bus

Connections and buses

CAN bus is a sturdy wired network made for cars. It lets many controllers share two wires. Robots and machines use it too.

Why tinkerers care: CAN bus lets a project read car data or link robot motors over long cables.

Related terms: Bus, Motor driver, UART

Appears on 9 entries.

Debug probe

Connections and buses

A debug probe is a small tool that connects a computer to a chip's insides. It lets you pause code and look at values step by step. JTAG and SWD are common debug links.

Why tinkerers care: A debug probe turns hours of guessing into minutes of finding the bug.

Related terms: Flashing, IDE, Firmware

Appears on 19 entries.

Ethernet

Connections and buses

Ethernet is wired networking through a network cable. It is steady and fast. Many SBCs have an Ethernet jack.

Why tinkerers care: Ethernet keeps a server or camera online with fewer dropouts than Wi-Fi.

Related terms: Wi-Fi, Single-board computer

Appears on 32 entries.

Grove

Connections and buses

Grove is a plug-in system from Seeed Studio. Its four-pin plugs carry I2C, UART, analog, or digital signals. The plug is larger than Qwiic.

Why tinkerers care: Grove kits let classrooms build projects with no soldering or breadboards.

Related terms: Qwiic, STEMMA QT, I2C, JST connector

Appears on 14 entries.

HDMI

Connections and buses

HDMI is the common plug for TVs and monitors. It carries video and sound. Many SBCs have a smaller micro HDMI version.

Why tinkerers care: Check the HDMI plug size before buying. You may need an adapter cable.

Related terms: Display, MIPI DSI, Single-board computer

Appears on 31 entries.

I2C

Connections and buses

I2C is a simple way for chips to talk over two wires. Many sensors can share the same two wires. Each part has its own address.

Why tinkerers care: I2C lets you chain several sensors with very little wiring.

Related terms: Qwiic, STEMMA QT, Grove, SPI, Bus

Appears on 7 entries.

I2S

Connections and buses

I2S is a way for chips to pass digital sound. It links boards to microphones, amplifiers, and audio chips. The sound stays clean along the way.

Why tinkerers care: I2S parts give a small board much better sound than a plain buzzer.

Related terms: MEMS microphone, Amplifier, Audio codec, DAC

JST connector

Connections and buses

JST is a brand name used for many small plastic plugs. Batteries and plug-in sensors often use them. Plugs that look alike can still differ in size.

Why tinkerers care: Matching the exact JST type and wire order avoids a fried board.

Related terms: Qwiic, LiPo battery, Grove

Jumper wire

Connections and buses

A jumper wire is a short wire with a pin or socket on each end. It links a board to a breadboard or a part. Kits come with many colors.

Why tinkerers care: A bag of jumper wires is one of the first things every tinkerer needs.

Related terms: Breadboard, Pin header

Appears on 7 entries.

MIPI CSI

Connections and buses

MIPI CSI is a fast link between a camera and a processor. It usually uses a thin flat ribbon cable. Many SBCs have one or two CSI ports.

Why tinkerers care: A CSI port gives sharp, fast video for AI vision projects.

Related terms: Camera module, MIPI DSI, Single-board computer

Appears on 14 entries.

MIPI DSI

Connections and buses

MIPI DSI is a fast link between a processor and a screen. It also uses a flat ribbon cable. Official touchscreens for SBCs often use it.

Why tinkerers care: A DSI screen keeps the HDMI port free and makes a neat built-in display.

Related terms: Display, MIPI CSI, HDMI

Appears on 2 entries.

PCIe

Connections and buses
