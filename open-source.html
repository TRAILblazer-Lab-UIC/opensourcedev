What Is Open Source?

Think of a family recipe. Some families keep their recipes secret. Other families write them on a card and hand out copies. Anyone can cook the dish. Anyone can add more garlic and share the new version.

Open source works like the shared recipe card. The makers publish the plans. Anyone may study them, build them, change them, and share them.

Open-source software

Software is the set of instructions that tells a machine what to do. Open-source software publishes those instructions for anyone to read. A license spells out the rules for reuse. Popular licenses include MIT, Apache, and GPL.

You already use open-source software every day. It runs most websites and nearly every phone.

Open-source hardware

Hardware is the physical gadget. Its plans include drawings of the circuit, called schematics. They also include the board layout files that a factory uses to make it. Open-source hardware publishes these plans under an open license.

This openness matters for tinkerers. You can see exactly how a board works. You can fix it, copy it, or build a custom version. Small companies around the world can sell their own versions. That competition keeps prices low.

The Open Source Hardware Association runs a free certification program. Certified boards carry a small gear logo. This guide marks those boards.

Why nothing is perfectly open

Almost every board uses a chip from a big company. The inside of that chip is usually secret. Some boards also need a small closed driver to run Wi-Fi or graphics. A few new chips do publish their inner design. This guide marks those with an "open silicon" note.

What open source gives you

  • You can learn from real designs used by real products.
  • You can find help in large and friendly online communities.
  • You can avoid getting stuck with one company.
  • Your projects can keep working after a company moves on.
  • You can share your own improvements with everyone.

Words to know

  • Open source means the plans are public and free to reuse under a license.
  • A license is the set of rules for using and sharing the plans.
  • A schematic is a drawing of how the parts connect.
  • Design files are the full plans a factory needs to make the board.
  • A fork is a copy of a project that someone changes and shares.
