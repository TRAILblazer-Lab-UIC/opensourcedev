Overview

The ADALM-Pluto is a software defined radio from Analog Devices. It turns a laptop into a radio lab that can both receive and transmit between 325 MHz and 3.8 GHz. It was made for university courses on radio and signals. It suits students and hobbyists who want to learn how radio really works.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning radio and digital communication in a class or at home
  • Receiving and decoding public signals such as airplane position messages
  • Safe transmit practice over the included loopback cable
  • Python, GNU Radio, and MATLAB experiments
  • Studying open board files and open Linux firmware

Not great for

  • Low frequencies such as FM broadcast radio, which sit below its 325 MHz floor
  • Wide live streams, as USB 2.0 caps gap-free data near 4 MSPS
  • Precise frequency work on early units with a basic crystal
  • Casual transmitting, which needs a license in most bands

Specs in plain words

  • 325 MHz to 3.8 GHz covers airplane signals, many home sensors, and the 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi band.
  • 20 MHz of bandwidth lets it see a slice of the airwaves about as wide as several Wi-Fi channels at once.
  • One transmit and one receive port can work at the same time, which is called full duplex.
  • 12-bit samples capture weak and strong signals with good detail.
  • 512 MB of memory and a 667 MHz Arm core run a small Linux system inside the box.

RF is 325 MHz to 3.8 GHz, 200 kHz to 20 MHz bandwidth, 12-bit, 65.1 kSPS to 61.44 MSPS. USB 2.0 streams up to about 4 MSPS without drops. No radio vocabulary value fits a general SDR. As a result, radios is empty. EVM is about -40 dB. No TCXO on early units, about 25 ppm accuracy per RTL-SDR.com. UART, I2C and SPI breakout pins and U.FL second RX and TX channels appear only on Rev D. The second channel is not production tested. The USB host mode uses the OTG port. The USB connector type and enclosure size were not confirmed.

Things you could build

13 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • See the radio crowd at home

    Sweep across the 2.4 GHz band and draw a live picture of how busy it is. Find a quieter channel for your own Wi-Fi.

  • Safe loopback lab

    Connect transmit to receive with the included SMA cable. Send a test tone through the wire and watch it arrive with no signal going on the air.

  • Spot noisy home gadgets

    Scan the bands near your desk while you switch devices on and off. Find which charger or light leaks radio noise.

Builder ideas

  • Watch airplanes overhead

    Tune to 1090 MHz and decode the public position messages that airliners broadcast. Plot nearby flights on a map.

    Add: 1090 MHz antenna.

  • Read your own weather station

    Pick up the 433 MHz signals from your own outdoor weather sensor. Log the temperature on your computer.

    Add: 433 MHz antenna.

  • Learn modulation in class

    Build AM, FM, and digital signals in GNU Radio over the loopback cable. Students see each wave shape on screen.

  • Python signal explorer

    Grab raw samples in Python with pyadi-iio. Plot the spectrum and try your own filters.

    Uses: Runs Linux.

  • Test your own antennas

    Compare homemade antennas by watching signal strength from a known local broadcast. Pick the best design for your project.

    Add: homemade antennas, SMA adapters.

  • MATLAB and Simulink coursework

    Use the MATLAB support package to stream real samples into lab assignments. Turn textbook ideas into live signals.

Expert ideas

  • Radio astronomy hydrogen hunt

    Listen near 1420 MHz for the faint hiss of hydrogen gas in the Milky Way. A dish or horn antenna points at the sky.

    Add: horn or dish antenna, low noise amplifier.

  • Licensed ham radio experiments

    Hold an amateur radio license first. Then send and receive on your licensed bands with low power and your call sign.

    Add: antenna for your band, band filter.

  • Custom FPGA signal blocks

    Rebuild the Pluto firmware with your own logic in the FPGA. Move heavy signal math off the laptop.

    Uses: Runs Linux.

  • Standalone radio logger

    Run a small program on the Pluto's own Linux. Save signal levels from your own sensor network to a USB stick.

    Uses: Runs Linux, Plugs in USB gadgets. Add: USB flash drive, USB OTG adapter.

How open is it?

Open Board

Analog Devices publishes schematics, Gerbers, parts lists, and board files for several revisions. The firmware mixes GPL-2.0, LGPL, BSD, and Apache code, and libiio uses LGPL-2.1. No hardware license is stated, and the radio chip is closed. Changing the FPGA design needs the closed Vivado tools.

Hardware license: None stated · Software license: Firmware mix of GPL-2.0+, LGPL-2.0+, BSD and Apache, libiio LGPL-2.1, HDL dual ADI BSD or GPL-2.0 per module
