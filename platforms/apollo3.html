Apollo3

Ambiq

An ultra-low-power Bluetooth chip that powers SparkFun's Artemis boards.

Field marks: The Apollo3 hides on SparkFun Artemis boards behind a pinhole microphone, draws about one microamp in deep sleep, and answers to four names, including AMA3B1KK.

Overview

The Apollo3 Blue is a tiny chip from Ambiq that sips power and has Bluetooth built in. Most hobbyists meet it on SparkFun's Artemis boards. It suits battery projects, sensor loggers, and small voice or gesture models. Its standout trait is very low power use for a fast chip.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Battery sensors that need to run for weeks
  • Bluetooth links to a phone
  • Sound projects with the onboard microphone
  • Tiny machine learning such as spotting a spoken word
  • Plug-in sensors over the Qwiic port with no soldering

Not great for

  • Wi-Fi projects, as the chip has no Wi-Fi radio
  • Picking from many board makers, as almost every board comes from SparkFun
  • Long-term support, as the Arduino core builds on Mbed OS with an unclear future

Specs in plain words

  • The 48 MHz speed can burst to 96 MHz. That is plenty for sensors and small sound models.
  • 1 MB of flash holds a big program plus a small trained model.
  • 384 KB of working memory fits a short slice of sound for word spotting.
  • About 1 microamp in deep sleep means a small battery can last a very long time.
  • A digital microphone input lets many boards hear sound with no extra parts.
  • A Qwiic port lets you click in sensors with one small cable.

Nominal clock is 48 MHz and TurboSPOT raises it to 96 MHz. The 96 MHz value is the burst figure. GPIO is 50 on the 81-pin BGA, 37 on the 66-pin WLCSP and 38 on the 64-pin QFN. The Artemis module breaks out 48 GPIO. Boards expose fewer pins, such as 24 on the RedBoard Artemis and 17 on the Artemis Nano. The ADC has 15 channels on the chip. By contrast, boards expose 8 to 10. The chip has six I2C/SPI masters, one I2C/SPI slave, one MSPI, two UARTs, PDM and one I2S slave. The 1 uA figure is deep sleep with RTC and BLE off at 3.3 V, per the Ambiq page. Connectors and onboard items describe the common SparkFun boards, which use USB-C, Qwiic and a PDM microphone. The Apollo3 Blue Plus is a separate part with 768 KB SRAM and 74 GPIO.

Things you could build

Starter ideas

  • Bluetooth plant thermometer

    Read a temperature sensor and send the value to your phone over Bluetooth. Check the greenhouse from the garden path.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: temperature sensor.

  • Clap counter for the classroom

    Use the built-in microphone to count loud claps. Students test how many claps fit in ten seconds.

    Uses: Hears sound, Sensors built in.

  • Noise level meter

    Measure how loud a room gets and light a green, yellow or red LED. A library or lunchroom gets a gentle reminder.

    Uses: Hears sound, Sensors built in. Add: three LEDs.

  • Weather logger with plug-in sensors

    Snap a weather sensor onto the Qwiic port and log readings over many days. No soldering is needed.

    Uses: Plug-in connectors, Sips power. Add: Qwiic weather sensor, LiPo battery.

  • Soil moisture alarm

    Check the soil every hour and beep on a buzzer once it gets dry. Your houseplants get a voice.

    Uses: Sips power. Add: soil moisture sensor, buzzer.

  • Fridge door reminder

    Sense an open door with a magnet switch and beep after one minute. Send an alert to your phone too.

    Uses: Bluetooth. Add: magnetic door switch, buzzer.

Builder ideas

  • Step counter wearable

    Read a motion sensor and count steps through the day. Send the total to your phone over Bluetooth.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: motion sensor, small LiPo battery.

  • Bird feeder visit counter

    Use a motion sensor to count each bird visit. The board sleeps between visits and runs for weeks on a battery.

    Uses: Sips power. Add: motion sensor, LiPo battery, weatherproof box.

  • Bluetooth game controller

    Wire a joystick and a few buttons. Send moves to a phone game over Bluetooth.

    Uses: Bluetooth. Add: joystick module, push buttons.

  • Simple tone synthesizer

    Read a few buttons and knobs and play tones through a small speaker. Make a pocket noise box for music class.

    Uses: Makes sound. Add: small speaker, amplifier board, push buttons, knobs.

  • Heart rate logger

    Plug in a pulse sensor and log beats per minute during a walk. Look at the numbers on your phone afterwards.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Plug-in connectors. Add: Qwiic pulse sensor.

  • Wildlife sound detector

    Listen for loud sounds at night and log the time of each one. See when the backyard gets busy.

    Uses: Hears sound, Sips power. Add: microSD card module, LiPo battery.

Expert ideas

  • Spoken word light switch

    Train a tiny speech model to hear "yes" and "no". Turn a lamp on and off with your voice.

    Uses: Hears sound. Add: relay module, lamp.

  • Gesture wand

    Train a small model to tell a circle from a flick using a motion sensor. Each gesture fires a different light effect.

    Add: motion sensor, LED strip.

How open is it?

Openness level

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansFull design files
  • Hardware licenseOpen license
  • Coding toolsMostly open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

SparkFun shares the schematics and board files for its Artemis boards under CC BY-SA 4.0. Many of these boards are certified open hardware. The Ambiq software kit uses a BSD-style license, but the Bluetooth radio firmware ships as a closed file. The chip itself is a closed design.
