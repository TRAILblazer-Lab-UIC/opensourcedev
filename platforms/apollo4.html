Overview

Apollo4 is a family of microcontrollers from Ambiq that sip very little power. They run inside smartwatches, fitness bands, and always-listening voice gadgets. Most parts include a graphics engine for round watch screens, and the Blue models add Bluetooth. It suits experienced developers who want to study low-power wearables.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Wearables that run for a long time on a small battery
  • Round watch screens with smooth graphics
  • Always-on voice and sound detection
  • Studying small AI models with Ambiq's neuralSPOT kit

Not great for

  • Beginners, as all code is in C with pro tools
  • Tight budgets, as boards cost about 150 to 260 US dollars
  • Wi-Fi projects, as it has no Wi-Fi
  • Plugging in common 3.3 volt parts on custom boards, as the chip runs near 2 volts

Specs in plain words

  • A 192 MHz processor is fast enough for watch graphics and small AI models.
  • 2.75 MB of working memory holds screen images and sound data at the same time.
  • 2 MB of MRAM stores your program and keeps it with the power off.
  • A sleep draw of about 1.5 microamps lets a coin cell last a very long time between tasks.
  • The graphics engine drives round watch screens up to 454 by 454 pixels.
  • Bluetooth LE 5.4 on the Blue models links the chip to phones.

Values are for the Apollo4 Plus, the most common EVB part, with 2.75 MB SRAM, 2 MB MRAM and 105 GPIO on the 146-pin BGA. The original Apollo4 has 1.8 MB SRAM, and the Lite parts have 1.4 MB SRAM and 75 to 84 GPIO. BLE 5.4 is only on the Apollo4 Blue Plus and Blue Lite. USB is HS/FS on the original Apollo4 and FS on the Plus parts. The Lite parts have no USB and no DSI. The 1.5 uA sleep figure and the 3 uA per MHz launch figure come from CNX coverage of the original Apollo4. Ambiq lists 29 to 38 uW per MHz for later members. Connectors describe the EVB with USB-C, headers and buttons per CNX.

Things you could build

10 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Builder ideas

  • Classroom noise meter

    Measure sound level with a microphone and show it as a colored bar on a screen. Students can watch the room get quieter.

    Uses: Hears sound, Screen. Add: digital microphone, small display.

  • Bluetooth weather badge

    Read temperature and humidity and show them on a small screen. A phone app can read the values over Bluetooth.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Screen. Add: temperature and humidity sensor, small display.

  • Talking reminder tag

    Play a short recorded message at set times. It suits pill or chore reminders.

    Uses: Makes sound, Sips power. Add: small speaker, amplifier board.

  • Blink a light in Zephyr

    Build and flash the Zephyr blinky example on the evaluation board. It proves your toolchain works.

Expert ideas

  • DIY smartwatch face

    Draw a round watch face with hands and a date on a small screen. The graphics engine keeps the motion smooth.

    Uses: Screen, Sips power. Add: round display kit, small battery.

  • Wake word listener

    Listen through a microphone for one spoken word. The chip stays in low power until it hears it.

    Uses: Hears sound, Sips power. Add: digital microphone.

  • Heart rhythm demo

    Run the heartKIT demo to study ECG signals with a small AI model. It shows how wearables spot heart patterns.

    Uses: Sips power. Add: ECG sensor board, electrodes.

  • Bluetooth step counter

    Count steps with a motion sensor and send the total to a phone over Bluetooth. It can run for a long time on a coin cell.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: motion sensor board, coin cell holder.

  • Sleep tracker band

    Log motion through the night and show a sleep score in the morning. The low sleep current keeps the battery going.

    Uses: Sips power. Add: motion sensor board, small battery.

  • Bird song detector

    Train a small model to spot one bird call. Log each detection with the time of day.

    Uses: Hears sound, Sips power. Add: digital microphone, weatherproof case, battery pack.

How open is it?

Level 1 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

The chip is closed, and no schematics were found for Ambiq's evaluation boards. The software is mostly open, with neuralSPOT under BSD-3-Clause and Ambiq's Zephyr code under Apache-2.0. The Bluetooth firmware and the J-Link host software stay closed.
