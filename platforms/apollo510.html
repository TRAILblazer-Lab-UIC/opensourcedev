Overview

The Apollo510 is a microcontroller chip from Ambiq built for wearables and earbuds. It runs small AI models on very little power. It suits engineers and advanced hobbyists who want to test voice, sound, or gesture models. Its standout trait is a fast Cortex-M55 core with vector math and a graphics engine.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Always-on voice and sound detection
  • Testing tiny AI models with Edge Impulse or neuralSPOT
  • Smooth graphics on small watch-style screens
  • Measuring how much power an AI model uses

Not great for

  • Beginners, as all code is C with an SDK or Zephyr
  • Tight budgets, as the evaluation board costs about 210 to 260 dollars
  • Wireless projects on the base chip, as only the Apollo510B adds Bluetooth
  • Hobby add-ons, as no maker boards use this chip yet

Specs in plain words

  • 250 MHz with vector math lets it crunch sound and motion models quickly.
  • Up to 3.75 MB of working memory holds a sizable AI model plus a screen buffer.
  • Up to 4 MB of storage fits your program and the trained model together.
  • A graphics engine draws smooth shapes on a screen up to 640 by 480 pixels.
  • Two digital microphone inputs let it listen with no analog parts.
  • The chip runs on less than 2.2 volts. Custom boards need a level shifter for common 3.3 volt sensors.

PowerUSB-C on the EVB. The chip runs from 1.71 to 2.2 V

Values are for the base Apollo510 on the BGA package. The CSP package has 144 GPIO, and the Apollo510 Lite has 2 MB NVM, 2 MB SRAM and up to 120 GPIO. The Zephyr EVB target gives 2 MiB of RAM and 4032 KiB of flash to applications. The base Apollo510 has no radio. The Apollo510B and 510B Lite add BLE 5.4 and the 510D Lite adds Bluetooth Classic. The ADC is 12-bit with 11 channels, listed at 1.7 MS/s in the spec section and 2.8 MS/s in Ambiq's table. Ambiq does not publish uA per MHz or sleep figures on the product page. As a result, low_power_ua is null. Connectors describe the AP510EVB, which has USB-C, a mikroBUS socket and an onboard J-Link.

How open is it?

Openness level

Level 1 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansNot published
  • Hardware licenseNot confirmed
  • Coding toolsMostly open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

The chip is a closed design, and no schematics for the evaluation board were found. The software is mostly open. Zephyr support is Apache-2.0, and neuralSPOT is BSD-3-Clause. Flashing uses closed J-Link software, and the Bluetooth version runs closed radio firmware.
