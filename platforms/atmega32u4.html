ATmega32U4

Microchip Technology (formerly Atmel)

An 8-bit chip with built-in USB that can pretend to be a keyboard.

Field marks: Found under homemade keyboards, this 8-bit chip with 2.5 KB of RAM convinces any computer it meets that it is a perfectly normal USB keyboard.

Overview

The ATmega32U4 is an 8-bit chip from Microchip, first made by Atmel. Its standout trick is built-in USB. A board can act as a keyboard, mouse, or MIDI music device with no extra chip. It powers the Arduino Leonardo and Micro, the SparkFun Pro Micro, and several Adafruit boards.

How the scores work

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Custom keyboards and macro pads with the open QMK firmware
  • Game controllers and arcade sticks
  • USB MIDI music gear
  • Shortcut buttons and accessibility switches
  • Small projects on breadboards with tiny boards

Not great for

  • Wireless projects on most boards, as the chip has no radio
  • Big screens, sound, or AI with their large memory needs
  • Total beginners on cheap clones, which can stop showing up on the computer after a bad upload

Specs in plain words

  • 16 MHz is a modest speed. It handles one simple job at a time.
  • 32 KB of flash holds a program of a few hundred lines.
  • 2.5 KB of working memory is tiny. Long text and screen images fill it fast.
  • Built-in USB lets the board talk to a computer as a keyboard, mouse or music device.
  • 26 input and output lines are enough for a full keyboard grid.
  • 5 volt boards run at 16 MHz and 3.3 volt boards run at 8 MHz.
Full spec sheet
Processor8-bit AVR (megaAVR) with USB, 1 core at 16 MHz
Memory2.5 KB
Storage32 KB
Pins26 pins, 12 analog inputs
Ways to connect partsI2C, SPI, Serial (UART), USB device, Debug port
ConnectorsMicro USB, Pin headers
Built inbuttons
Power5 V over USB on the Pro Micro and Leonardo

Values are for the chip on a 5 V, 16 MHz board such as the Leonardo or Pro Micro. The chip runs at 8 MHz on 3.3 V boards such as the Feather 32u4. The Leonardo and Micro use micro-USB, and the SparkFun Qwiic Pro Micro uses USB-C. The ItsyBitsy 32u4 measures 35 x 18 mm. The datasheet features page gives no power-down current. As a result, low_power_ua is null. swd_jtag reflects the chip's JTAG debug port.

Things you could build

12 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • One-button password typer

    Press a single button and the board types a long phrase for you. It acts just like a USB keyboard.

    Add: large push button.

  • Video call mute button

    Wire a big button that sends the mute shortcut to your computer. Quiet a call with one slap.

    Add: arcade button.

  • Macro pad for shortcuts

    Wire a small grid of keys for copy, paste, and other shortcuts. Each key fires a whole shortcut at once.

    Add: keyboard switches, keycaps.

  • Slide clicker for teachers

    Wire two buttons as the next and back keys. Move through slides from across the classroom on a long cable.

    Add: push buttons, long USB cable.

  • Sensor data straight to a spreadsheet

    Read a temperature sensor and type each value into an open spreadsheet. Students get a live table with no extra software.

    Add: temperature sensor.

Builder ideas

  • Homemade game controller

    Wire a joystick and some buttons. The computer sees a ready-to-use game pad.

    Add: joystick module, arcade buttons.

  • Accessible mouse for limited movement

    Turn large foot pedals or a head switch into mouse clicks. People who cannot use a regular mouse get another way in.

    Add: foot pedal switches, large push buttons.

  • Tilt mouse

    Read a motion sensor and move the mouse pointer as you tilt the board. Wave your hand to steer the cursor.

    Add: motion sensor.

  • USB MIDI knob box

    Wire a row of knobs that send MIDI messages over USB. Control volume and effects in your music program.

    Add: knobs, project box.

  • Drum pads for music class

    Read piezo pads and send drum notes over USB MIDI. Students play beats on a laptop music app.

    Add: piezo pads, resistors.

  • Retro arcade stick

    Build a wooden box with a joystick and eight buttons. Play old arcade games on your computer.

    Add: arcade joystick, arcade buttons, wooden box.

Expert ideas

  • Custom split keyboard

    Build a two-piece keyboard with one board per half and load the open QMK firmware. Lay out every key the way your hands like.

    Add: keyboard switches, keycaps, keyboard circuit board, cable between halves.

SparkFun shares the Pro Micro design files under CC BY-SA 4.0, and Adafruit and Arduino share theirs too. Several of these boards are certified open hardware. The compiler, uploader, and QMK firmware are open source. The chip itself is a closed Microchip design.
