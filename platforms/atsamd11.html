Overview

The SAM D11 is a tiny 32-bit chip from Microchip, first released by Atmel. The 14-pin version is easy to hand-solder. It can act as a USB keyboard, mouse, or serial port with no extra crystal. Fab Academy students and makers use it for minimal USB boards and debug probes.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Tiny USB gadgets such as shortcut keys and macro pads
  • Touch buttons and sliders made from copper pads
  • Homemade debug probes and USB serial adapters
  • Learning to design and solder your own board

Not great for

  • Big programs, as only about 12 KB of space is left for code
  • Absolute beginners, as a blank chip needs a debug probe first
  • Wireless projects, as it has no radio
  • Screens, sound, or cameras

Specs in plain words

  • 48 MHz is quick for such a tiny chip. It handles one simple job at a time.
  • 16 KB of flash holds a short program of a few hundred lines.
  • 4 KB of working memory is room for a few dozen sensor readings at once.
  • Full-speed USB lets the chip pose as a keyboard, mouse, or serial port.
  • The 14-pin chip is smaller than a fingernail.
  • Standby draws about 9 microamps and stretches a coin cell a long way.
Full spec sheet
ProcessorArm Cortex-M0+, 1 core at 48 MHz
Memory4 KB
Storage16 KB
Pins12 pins, 5 analog inputs
Ways to connect partsI2C, SPI, Serial (UART), USB device, Debug port
ConnectorsUSB-A, Pin headers
Power1.62 to 3.63 V for the chip
Deep sleep draw9.2 microamps

Values are for the 14-pin ATSAMD11C14A, the most common hobby part, from Atmel datasheet 42363H (09/2016). The 20-pin SOIC D11D part has 18 GPIO, 8 ADC channels and 3 SERCOMs. The 24-pin QFN part has 22 GPIO, 10 ADC channels and 3 SERCOMs. All parts have 16 KB flash, 4 KB SRAM, one 10-bit DAC, two analog comparators and a 6-channel DMA. Low-power value is the typical Standby current at 25 C and 3.3 V with the RTC and 32 kHz crystal stopped. With the MattairTech bootloader, 4 KB of flash is used, leaving about 12 KB for sketches. Connectors vary by board. Many hobby boards use a PCB-edge USB-A plug or USB-C.

Things you could build

10 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Builder ideas

  • One-button shortcut key

    Wire a big button to the chip and make it act as a USB keyboard. One press types your password manager hotkey or mutes a call.

    Add: large push button.

  • Touch slider volume knob

    Lay out copper pads as a touch slider with the built-in touch controller. Slide a finger to raise or lower computer volume.

    Add: copper tape.

  • Mini macro pad

    Wire a few keys to the pins and plug the board into a laptop. Each key fires a shortcut for drawing or video editing.

    Add: keyboard switches, keycaps.

  • USB mood light

    Plug a tiny USB stick board into a computer. A color LED glows or fades on command from the computer.

    Add: RGB LED, resistors.

  • Homemade USB serial adapter

    Turn the chip into a USB to serial bridge. Use it to talk to other boards from a computer.

    Add: jumper wires.

  • Mouse jiggler

    Make the chip pose as a USB mouse. It nudges the pointer every few minutes. It keeps a display computer awake.

  • Plug-in temperature reader

    Add a small temperature sensor and send readings over USB serial. A computer graphs room temperature through the day.

    Add: temperature sensor.

  • Coin cell sensor node

    Read a sensor once a minute and blink a light on alarm. The chip sleeps on a few microamps between checks.

    Uses: Sips power. Add: temperature sensor, coin cell holder, LED.

Expert ideas

  • Open debug probe

    Build a free-dap board and flash its firmware. It becomes a CMSIS-DAP probe for programming other Arm chips.

    Add: printed circuit board, soldering iron.

  • Mill your own USB board

    Mill a Fab Academy style board on a desktop mill and solder the 14-pin chip. Flash a bootloader and run Arduino sketches on it.

    Add: copper board blank, desktop mill, soldering iron, voltage regulator.

How open is it?

Openness level

Level 3 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

Several community boards share full design files, such as the wagiminator board under CC BY-SA 3.0 and free-dap under BSD-3-Clause. Code builds with the free arm-none-eabi-gcc compiler and flashes with open tools. The MattairTech core lists no clear license. The chip itself is a closed Microchip design.
