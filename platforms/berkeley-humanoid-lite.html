Berkeley Humanoid Lite

UC Berkeley Hybrid Robotics (Sreenath lab) and Berkeley Architecture Research

An open, 3D-printed humanoid robot from UC Berkeley for walking research.

Field marks: Look for a waist-high stack of PLA gearboxes, 22 motor drivers, four USB-CAN adapters, and a parts spreadsheet north of four thousand dollars.

Overview

Berkeley Humanoid Lite is an open humanoid robot from UC Berkeley. It stands about 0.8 meters tall and has 22 joints made from 3D-printed gearboxes and drone motors. A small Intel mini PC in the chest runs walking skills learned in simulation. It suits university labs and very determined makers.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Research on how robots learn to walk
  • Learning to build strong joints from printed parts
  • VR remote control experiments
  • Team builds in a university robotics club

Not great for

  • Beginners, as the build takes about a week and uses high-power electronics
  • Small budgets, as the parts add up to about 4,312 dollars in the US
  • Anyone without an NVIDIA graphics card for training
  • Buying ready-made, as no kit or assembled robot is sold

Specs in plain words

  • 22 joints give it five moving joints per arm and six per leg, close to a person's main joints.
  • 16 kilograms is about as heavy as a large bag of dog food.
  • All printed parts fit a common desktop printer with a 200 millimeter bed.
  • A 6S 4000 mAh battery pack gives about 30 minutes of running time.
  • Four CAN buses act like four separate wired phone lines, one for each limb.

The paper gives 16 kg and 0.8 m height. Berkeley Engineering news says about 1 m. Joint map from the docs has 22 joints (shoulder pitch, roll and yaw plus elbow pitch and yaw per arm. Hip roll, yaw and pitch, knee, ankle pitch and roll per leg). Ten 6512 actuators use MAD M6C12 150KV motors. Twelve 5010 actuators use MAD 5010 motors. Each has a magnetic encoder. Each limb is its own 1 Mbps CAN 2.0 bus, linked to the PC through four USB-CAN adapters. Control runs at 250 Hz. The N95 core count and clock and the mini PC RAM were not on a fetched page. The STM32G431 part name comes from the ST B-G431B-ESC1 board name. It is not a fetched datasheet.

Things you could build

10 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Builder ideas

  • Print one test actuator

    Print and build a single cycloidal actuator before committing to the full robot. It shows how a cheap drone motor becomes a strong joint.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: 3D printer, brushless motor, motor driver board.

Expert ideas

  • Robot arm from spare joints

    Chain a few printed actuators into a desk arm. Use it to wave, point, or stack light blocks.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: 3D printer, brushless motors, motor driver boards, USB-CAN adapter.

  • Simulated walking lab

    Train a walking policy in simulation and watch the robot learn to step. Test it in a second simulator before trying real hardware.

    Uses: Runs Linux. Add: computer with a graphics card.

  • First steps on real legs

    Load a trained policy onto the mini PC and let the full robot walk. A safety rope from an overhead frame keeps it off the floor.

    Uses: Runs Linux, Drives motors, Battery ready. Add: overhead safety frame and rope, battery charger.

  • VR puppet robot

    Wear a VR headset and move your arms to steer the robot's arms. It is a fun demo for open house days.

    Uses: Runs Linux, Drives motors, Plugs in USB gadgets. Add: VR headset.

  • Balance and tilt study

    Read the motion sensor while you nudge the robot. Plot how fast it corrects its tilt.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Runs Linux.

  • Gripper pick and place

    Add the two printed grippers and teach the robot to pick up a soft toy. Practice moving it from one table to another.

    Uses: Drives motors, Runs Linux. Add: printed grippers.

  • Joint wear testing rig

    Run one actuator back and forth for hours and record how it holds up. The project has no published lifetime numbers yet.

    Uses: Drives motors, Plugs in USB gadgets. Add: USB-CAN adapter, power supply.

  • University robotics club build

    Split the 22 actuators among club members as a team build. Each person learns printing, wiring, and flashing.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: 3D printers, soldering iron.

  • Battery life tracker

    Log battery voltage during each walking test. Learn how much each new gait drains the pack.

    Uses: Battery ready, Runs Linux.

How open is it?

Openness level

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Hardware licenseOpen license

  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

The code is MIT licensed, and the CAD, print files, and robot models are CC BY-SA 4.0. The electronics are off-the-shelf boards, and no custom circuit board is published. Training relies on NVIDIA Isaac Sim, and motor flashing uses a free but closed ST tool.
