Overview

The CC2340 is a cheap Bluetooth Low Energy chip from Texas Instruments. It also speaks Zigbee and Thread for smart home gadgets. It sips so little power that a coin cell can run it for a long time. It suits engineers and experienced makers more than beginners.

How the scores work

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Beacons, tags, and remote buttons that run for months on a coin cell
  • Zigbee and Thread smart home sensors
  • Learning how professional low-power wireless products are built
  • Studying TI's complete board design files

Not great for

  • Beginners, with no USB port and a separate debugger required
  • Hobby projects that need lots of tutorials or add-on boards
  • Heavy tasks such as screens, audio, or cameras
  • Bluetooth projects in Zephyr, which lacks radio support for this chip

Specs in plain words

  • A 48 MHz Arm Cortex-M0+ core is a simple, efficient brain for reading sensors and sending small messages.
  • Up to 512 KB of flash holds the radio software plus your program.
  • 36 to 64 KB of RAM is enough for a sensor tag but too little for big data or graphics.
  • A standby current under 0.71 microamps lets a coin cell last a very long time.
  • The 12-bit ADC reads analog sensors such as light or soil moisture with fine detail.
  • The chip has no USB. You load code through a debug probe.

Values are for the CC2340R52 (512 KB flash, 36 KB RAM), the chip on the original LP-EM-CC2340R5 LaunchPad. The CC2340R53 has 64 KB RAM, the CC2340R22 has 256 KB flash and 36 KB RAM, and the CC2340R21 has 256 KB flash and 28 KB RAM. GPIO is 26 on the QFN40 and 12 on the QFN24 package. Interfaces are one SPI, one UART and one I2C, plus a low-power comparator and timers. There is no USB. Low power is the datasheet standby figure with RTC and full RAM retention. Shutdown is 165 nA. The LaunchPad has two user LEDs, two buttons and BoosterPack headers per Zephyr. Size was not found.

Things you could build

11 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Builder ideas

  • Bluetooth key finder tag

    Build a small beacon that clips to your keys. Your phone can show how close it is.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: coin cell battery holder.

  • Classroom door beacon

    Put a beacon by each classroom door. A phone app can tell visitors which room they are near.

    Uses: Bluetooth. Add: coin cell battery holder.

  • Plant soil moisture tag

    Read a soil sensor every hour and send the result over Bluetooth. One coin cell can last a very long time.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: soil moisture sensor, coin cell battery holder.

  • Fridge temperature logger

    Check the fridge temperature every few minutes and send it to your phone. You learn quickly about a door left open.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: temperature sensor.

  • Bluetooth LED blinker

    Toggle the LaunchPad LEDs from a phone app. It is a good first test of the radio.

    Uses: Bluetooth.

  • Pet water bowl monitor

    Measure the water level in a pet bowl and warn your phone when it runs low.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: water level sensor.

Expert ideas

  • Wireless light switch remote

    Build a battery button that turns lamps on over Zigbee. Stick it on any wall with no wiring.

    Uses: Thread or Zigbee, Sips power. Add: push button, coin cell battery holder.

  • Zigbee door sensor

    Add a magnetic switch to a door or window. Your smart home hub hears each open and close.

    Uses: Thread or Zigbee, Sips power. Add: magnetic reed switch.

  • Thread mailbox alert

    Detect the mailbox lid opening and send a message over a Thread network. It saves you trips to the curb.

    Uses: Thread or Zigbee, Sips power, Mesh messaging. Add: magnetic reed switch.

  • Step counter wristband

    Pair the chip with a motion sensor to count steps. Send the total to your phone each evening.

    Uses: Bluetooth, Sips power. Add: accelerometer, small battery.

  • Greenhouse sensor mesh

    Spread several sensor nodes across a greenhouse and let them pass readings along a mesh network. Each node runs on batteries for months.

    Uses: Thread or Zigbee, Mesh messaging, Sips power. Add: temperature and humidity sensor.

How open is it?

Openness level

Open CodeOpen BoardWild Open

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansFull design files
  • Hardware licenseNot confirmed
  • Coding toolsMostly open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

TI publishes a full design package for the LaunchPad with schematics, board files, Gerbers, and a parts list. These files come under TI's own terms and carry no open hardware license. The SDK is on GitHub and builds with free GCC. Its license limits use to TI chips. The Zigbee stack ships as closed libraries.
