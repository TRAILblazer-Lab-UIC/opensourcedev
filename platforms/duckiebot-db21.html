Overview

The Duckiebot DB21 is a small self-driving car kit from the Duckietown project. Duckietown began as a class at MIT. The robot runs on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano and sees the road through one wide camera. It suits university and advanced high school classes that teach robot autonomy and AI with a full course.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning how self-driving cars see lanes and signs
  • University robotics and AI courses with a ready curriculum
  • Practicing ROS and Docker on a real robot
  • Training small vision models on the robot itself

Not great for

  • Beginners, as setup needs Ubuntu, Docker, and command line tools
  • Tight budgets, as the cheapest kit starts at about 429 dollars without the Jetson
  • Commercial products, as the software terms forbid commercial use
  • Quick builds, as the DIY kit takes three to four hours to put together

Specs in plain words

  • The Jetson Nano has four processor cores and 4 GB of memory. It works like a small computer that runs Linux.
  • Its 128-core graphics chip runs vision models fast enough to spot lanes and ducks while driving.
  • The 160 degree camera sees almost everything in front of the car. It acts as the robot's main eye.
  • Wheel encoders count each turn of the wheels. The robot can work out how far it drove.
  • The 10 Ah smart battery reports its charge live. It can shut the robot down safely when it runs low.
  • A 64 GB microSD card holds the operating system and your programs.

Core specs are for the Jetson Nano 4GB module (1.43 GHz, 4 GB LPDDR4, 472 GFLOPS, shown as 0.472 in ai_tops). The kit uses original NVIDIA Jetson Nano 4GB modules on third-party carrier boards and boots from a 64 GB microSD card. As a result, flash_kb is null. The Wi-Fi radio is a USB Wi-Fi dongle included in the box. The DB21M used a Jetson Nano 2GB and an MPU-9250 IMU. The DB21J moved to the MPU-6050 because of the 2021 to 2022 chip shortage. Interfaces listed are those of the Jetson Nano. The HUT uses I2C for the sensors and LEDs. Robot dimensions were not found.

Builder ideas

  • Lane-keeping drive

    Run the lane following demo on a taped road. The car reads the lines with its camera and steers itself.

    Uses: Camera, Drives motors, Runs Linux. Add: road tape, floor mats.

  • Keyboard joystick drive

    Drive the robot from a laptop keyboard over Wi-Fi. Watch the camera feed as you steer.

    Uses: Wi-Fi, Camera, Drives motors.

  • Stop sign reader

    Place AprilTag signs at a crossing. The car spots each tag and stops before turning.

    Uses: Camera, Runs Linux. Add: printed AprilTag signs.

  • Wheel odometry lesson

    Read the wheel encoders and estimate how far the car traveled. Compare the guess with a tape measure.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Drives motors. Add: tape measure.

  • Status screen messages

    Write a short program that shows battery level and mode on the small screen. Add a smiley face for good runs.

    Uses: Screen, Runs Linux.

  • Light signal language

    Blink the four color LEDs to show turns, stops, and parking. Classmates guess what the car will do next.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Runs Linux.

  • Obstacle stop

    Read the front distance sensor and halt before a box. Then plan a path around it.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Drives motors. Add: cardboard boxes.

  • Camera data collector

    Record camera images while you drive by hand. Use the pictures to build a training set for a class project.

    Uses: Camera, Runs Linux, Plugs in USB gadgets. Add: USB flash drive.

  • Wired lab setup

    Plug the car into an Ethernet cable on the bench. Update software fast without crowded Wi-Fi.

    Uses: Wired network, Runs Linux. Add: Ethernet cable.

Expert ideas

  • Duck crossing detector

    Train a small vision model to spot rubber ducks on the road. The car brakes for every duck in its path.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip, Runs Linux. Add: rubber ducks.

  • Model city traffic study

    Run several robots in one taped town. Students study how cars share lanes and crossings.

    Uses: Wi-Fi, Camera, Drives motors. Add: extra robots, road tape.

  • Simulator to street test

    Train a driving program in the free simulator first. Load it on the real car and see how well it transfers.

    Uses: Runs Linux, AI chip, Camera.

  • Parking spot finder

    Mark parking spots with tags and lines. The car searches the lot and backs in on its own.

    Uses: Camera, Drives motors, Runs Linux. Add: printed AprilTag signs, road tape.

  • Battery health log

    Read the smart battery's live data during long runs. Plot how fast it drains on hills and turns.

    Uses: Battery ready, Runs Linux.

  • Bump test with the motion sensor

    Use the motion sensor to sense bumps and sharp turns. Score each driving program on how smooth it rides.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Runs Linux.

The Duckietown software is public on GitHub. It falls under the Duckietown Software Terms of Use, which allow class and research use but forbid commercial use without a written agreement. No schematics or board files for the robot were found, and the Jetson relies on closed NVIDIA graphics drivers.
