ESP8266

Espressif Systems

The classic low-cost Wi-Fi chip behind countless hacked smart plugs and sensors.

Field marks: The ESP8266 launched as a five dollar Wi-Fi module in 2014, and it still hides in smart plugs and bulbs years after Espressif stamped it NRND.

Overview

The ESP8266 is a tiny Wi-Fi chip from Espressif that took the maker world by storm in 2014. It sits on cheap boards such as the NodeMCU and the D1 mini. It suits simple Wi-Fi gadgets like sensors, lights, and smart plugs. Espressif now marks it as a legacy chip and points new designs to newer ESP32 chips.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Simple Wi-Fi sensors that report to a phone
  • Smart plugs and lights running open firmware
  • LED strip and light projects
  • Cheap classroom Wi-Fi experiments

Not great for

  • New products meant to last for many years
  • Bluetooth projects, with no Bluetooth radio on board
  • Big programs or secure web connections with lots of data
  • Sound or camera projects

Specs in plain words

  • One core at 80 to 160 MHz is quick enough for Wi-Fi and a few sensors.
  • About 50 KB of free working memory fills up fast with large web pages or data.
  • 4 MB of storage on most boards holds your program and a few small files.
  • One analog input reads a single sensor such as a light sensor.
  • Deep sleep draws about 20 microamps. A battery can last for weeks between short wake-ups.

Chip values come from the ESP8266EX datasheet v7.1 and Wikipedia. Default clock is 80 MHz with 160 MHz available. The 160 KB RAM figure is total SRAM (32 KB IRAM, 32 KB cache, 96 KB DRAM). The datasheet says about 50 KB of heap is left in station mode. The chip has no internal flash. 4 MB is the common board value (Feather HUZZAH, D1 mini). I2C is done in software. The ADC input is limited to 1.0 V on the bare pin. Boards with a divider accept 3.2 to 3.3 V. Deep sleep is 20 uA per the datasheet. Shut down is 0.5 uA. Size is the NodeMCU DevKit v1.0 outline as commonly sold. The D1 mini is 34.2 x 25.6 mm.

How open is it?

Openness level

Open CodeOpen BoardWild Open

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansFull design files
  • Hardware licenseOpen license
  • Coding toolsMostly open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design
  • OSHWACertified open hardware

Many ESP8266 boards share full design files, such as the NodeMCU DevKit under MIT and the Adafruit and SparkFun boards under CC BY-SA. The Arduino core is open under LGPL-2.1 and the Espressif SDK is Apache-2.0. The Wi-Fi code ships as closed files, and the chip design itself stays closed.
