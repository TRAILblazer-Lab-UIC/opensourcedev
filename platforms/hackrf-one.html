Overview

HackRF One is an open hardware software defined radio from Great Scott Gadgets. It can receive or transmit anywhere from 1 MHz to 6 GHz, one direction at a time. Its full design files are public and OSHWA certified. It suits radio hobbyists, security learners, and teachers, though it now has a successor called the HackRF Pro.

How the scores work

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Exploring a huge range of the radio spectrum with one device
  • Receiving public signals such as FM radio, weather satellites, and airplane messages
  • Learning signals in GNU Radio and other SDR apps
  • Studying and modifying a fully open hardware design

Not great for

  • Buying new, as most US shops list it as discontinued
  • Sending and receiving at the same time, which it cannot do
  • Very weak or crowded signals, as 8-bit samples limit fine detail
  • Casual transmitting, which needs a license in most bands

Specs in plain words

  • 1 MHz to 6 GHz covers AM radio up through Wi-Fi and beyond.
  • 20 MSPS means it captures a slice of the airwaves about 20 MHz wide at once.
  • Half duplex means it either listens or talks, like a walkie-talkie.
  • Transmit power tops out near 15 dBm, about the strength of a small Wi-Fi gadget.
  • Input above -5 dBm can damage the receiver. Keep strong transmitters far away.
  • The case is about 124 by 80 millimeters and weighs about 100 grams.

The KiCad schematic names the LPC4320FBD144 MCU, W25Q80BV SPI flash (8 Mbit, recorded as 1024 KB), MAX5864 ADC/DAC, MAX2837 transceiver, RFFC5072 mixer and Si5351C clock. Revision r9 swaps the MAX2837 for the MAX2839. Cores counts the M4 and M0 in the LPC4320. Clock and SRAM were not confirmed from a fetched NXP page. No radio vocabulary value fits a general SDR. As a result, radios is empty. Max input power is -5 dBm. Size is for the plastic enclosure. Weight about 100 g.

Things you could build

16 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • Listen to FM radio in software

    Tune local FM stations in an SDR app on your laptop. Watch each station appear as a bright stripe on the waterfall display.

    Add: telescopic antenna.

  • Map the airwaves at home

    Sweep from low bands up to 6 GHz and chart which frequencies are busy. Make a poster of the invisible traffic around your house.

    Add: wideband antenna.

  • Hunt noisy chargers and lights

    Watch the spectrum while you switch your own gadgets on and off. Find the one that spills radio noise.

    Add: telescopic antenna.

Builder ideas

  • Catch weather satellite pictures

    Receive the 137 MHz signals from passing weather satellites. Turn the sound into a cloud picture of your region.

    Add: V-dipole antenna.

  • Track airplanes overhead

    Decode the public 1090 MHz position messages that airliners broadcast. Show the nearby flights on a map.

    Add: 1090 MHz antenna.

  • Read your own weather sensor

    Pick up the 433 MHz signal from your own outdoor weather sensor. Log the readings on your computer.

    Add: 433 MHz antenna.

  • Shortwave and AM listening post

    Tune broadcast stations from around the world in the low bands. Log the countries you hear in a notebook.

    Add: long wire antenna.

  • GNU Radio classroom labs

    Build receivers from blocks in GNU Radio and see each step of the signal. Students learn how an FM radio works inside.

  • Pocket receiver with a screen

    Add a third-party screen add-on and battery. Carry a handheld scanner for listening on hikes or field days.

    Add: screen add-on for HackRF, battery pack.

Expert ideas

  • Hydrogen line radio astronomy

    Point a horn antenna at the Milky Way and listen near 1420 MHz. Plot the faint hydrogen glow for a science fair.

    Add: horn antenna, low noise amplifier.

  • Licensed ham radio station

    Earn an amateur radio license first. Then transmit at low power on your licensed bands with your call sign.

    Add: antenna for your band, band filter.

  • Antenna switching experiments

    Add an antenna switching board and compare several antennas in one sweep. Find the best design for each band.

    Add: antenna switching board, several antennas.

  • Synced dual-radio setup

    Link two units through the clock ports. Record two receivers in step for direction or timing studies.

    Add: second HackRF One, SMA clock cable.

  • Write your own firmware

    Build the open firmware with GCC and load it over USB. Add a feature of your own to the radio.

  • Custom logic in the CPLD

    Change the CPLD design with Xilinx tools and test a new sample path. This suits deep hardware learners.

  • Build one from the open design

    Order a bare board made from the published KiCad files. Solder the parts and bring your own radio to life.

    Add: bare HackRF One circuit board, electronic parts kit, soldering station.

How open is it?

Openness level

Open CodeOpen BoardWild Open

Level 3 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansFull design files
  • Hardware licenseOpen license
  • Coding toolsFully open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design
  • OSHWACertified open hardware

HackRF One is very open. The KiCad schematics and board layout use the CERN-OHL-P-2.0 license, and the board is OSHWA certified as US000649. Firmware and host tools are GPL-2.0 and build with free tools. The radio chips are closed, and changing the CPLD logic needs Xilinx tools.
