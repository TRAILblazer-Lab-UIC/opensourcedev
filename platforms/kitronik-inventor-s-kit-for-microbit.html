Overview

The Kitronik Inventor's Kit is a breadboard kit for the BBC micro:bit. It has LEDs, a motor and fan, a dial, a light sensor, a buzzer and switches. A printed book walks you through ten experiments with no soldering. It suits classrooms, families and anyone who wants a first taste of electronics.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • First lessons in circuits with no soldering
  • Classrooms that already own micro:bits
  • Block coding in a web browser with MakeCode
  • A gentle path from blocks to Python

Not great for

  • Buyers without a micro:bit, which is sold separately
  • Big projects that need many sensors or long runs of wiring
  • Wi-Fi or internet projects, with no Wi-Fi on the micro:bit
  • US buyers who want quick shipping, with thin stock in late 2026

Specs in plain words

  • The micro:bit V2 chip with 128 KB of memory runs block programs and short Python scripts with ease.
  • The edge connector board brings 21 micro:bit pins out to the breadboard.
  • The breadboard holds parts in small holes with no soldering.
  • Six analog inputs can read dials and light sensors as smooth ranges of values.
  • The micro:bit V2 adds a speaker, microphone, motion sensor and compass on the board itself.

Specs describe the micro:bit V2 that the kit plugs into. The kit has no processor. Values are from the micro:bit V2.2 hardware page. The micro:bit V2 has 19 assignable GPIO and 6 analog inputs. The kit's edge connector board breaks out 21 pins. Onboard lists the micro:bit V2 parts (LED matrix, accelerometer and magnetometer, microphone, speaker, temperature) plus kit parts (LDR, push switches, RGB LED, piezo buzzer, motor). A micro:bit V1 uses an nRF51822 with 16 KB RAM and 256 KB flash and has no microphone or speaker.

Things you could build

14 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • Light level meter

    Read the light sensor from the kit and show the level on the micro:bit LEDs. Walk around the room to find the brightest spot.

    Uses: Screen.

  • Desk fan with a dial

    Drive the kit motor and fan through a transistor. Turn the dial to speed it up or slow it down.

  • Color mixing lamp

    Mix red, green and blue on the RGB LED with a turn of the dial. Learn how screens make every color.

  • Night light

    Switch on an LED once the room gets dark. It is a first taste of automatic gadgets.

  • Button piano

    Play notes on the buzzer with the push switches. Write a short tune and play it for the class.

    Uses: Makes sound.

  • Compass game

    Use the micro:bit compass to point players toward north. Hide a treasure and give directions by heading.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Screen.

  • Reaction time game

    Light an LED at a random moment and time how fast a player presses the switch. Show the score on the LED grid.

    Uses: Screen.

Builder ideas

  • Plant light checker

    Place the light sensor beside a houseplant for a day. Find out which window spot gets the most light.

    Uses: Battery ready, Screen. Add: battery pack.

  • Clap switch lamp

    Use the micro:bit V2 microphone to hear a clap and switch an LED. It feels like a smart home trick.

    Uses: Hears sound.

  • Classroom noise traffic light

    Measure the room sound level with the microphone. Light a green, yellow or red LED to show the volume.

    Uses: Hears sound.

  • Temperature alarm for a pet tank

    Read the micro:bit temperature sensor and sound the buzzer outside a safe range. Keep the board near the tank.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Makes sound, Battery ready. Add: battery pack.

  • Bluetooth fan remote

    Control the kit fan from a phone app over Bluetooth. Students see how a remote gadget works.

    Uses: Bluetooth.

  • Step counter science test

    Count steps with the motion sensor and compare walking and running. Graph the results in class.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Battery ready, Screen. Add: battery pack.

  • Python version of each experiment

    Rebuild the experiments with MicroPython code. It is a gentle bridge from blocks to text coding.

How open is it?

Open Code

The coding tools are open, with MakeCode under the MIT license and MicroPython also open source. Kitronik publishes no design files for the kit. The micro:bit has a published reference design, but its Bluetooth software is closed.

Hardware license: None published for the kit · Software license: MIT (Microsoft MakeCode pxt-microbit)
