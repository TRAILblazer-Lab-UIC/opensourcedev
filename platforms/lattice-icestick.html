Lattice iCEstick

Lattice Semiconductor

A USB stick with a small FPGA chip for learning digital hardware design.

Field marks: Look for a long bare circuit stick poking out of a laptop's USB port, blinking five tiny LEDs like a firefly on a branch.

Overview

The Lattice iCEstick is a small FPGA board shaped like a big USB thumb drive. An FPGA is a chip you rewire with code to become almost any digital circuit. This stick is famous as the first board to run a fully open FPGA toolchain. It suits students and hobbyists who want to learn Verilog and how digital logic works.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning digital logic and the Verilog language
  • Trying the open Yosys, nextpnr, and IceStorm tools
  • Following older open FPGA tutorials, which often target this board
  • Small experiments with LEDs, serial links, and Pmod add-ons

Not great for

  • Complete beginners, as it needs a hardware language
  • Bigger designs, as the chip has only 1280 logic cells
  • Wireless or analog sensor projects, as it has no radio or analog inputs
  • Tight budgets, as store prices in 2026 sit near 150 US dollars

Specs in plain words

  • 1280 logic cells is room for small circuits such as counters, a serial link or a very tiny processor.
  • About 8 KB of fast memory inside the chip holds a few short messages or a small table of data.
  • 4 MB of flash stores your design. The stick remembers it after you unplug.
  • A 12 MHz clock ticks twelve million times a second, and the chip can speed it up for faster designs.
  • One Pmod socket and 16 pin holes let you add buttons, displays and other parts.
  • The board is about 95 by 25 millimeters, about the shape of a chunky thumb drive.

clock_mhz is the 12 MHz Discera MEMS oscillator. The FPGA has a PLL. ram_kb is the 16 block RAMs of 4 Kbit each (64 Kbit, about 8 KB). flash_kb is the 32 Mbit N25Q32 SPI flash used to store the bitstream. gpio counts the 16 header I/O plus the 8 signals on the Pmod socket, matching the 24 free pins reported by Silicon Chip. The board also has an IrDA transceiver and five user LEDs, which have no matching onboard vocabulary entry. Size is the approximate figure from Silicon Chip.

Things you could build

11 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • Blink your first LED

    Write a tiny Verilog design that blinks one of the five LEDs. It shows how a chip can be rewired with code.

  • Spinning LED pattern

    Rebuild the classic rotating light example from the IceStorm docs. Then change the speed and the pattern yourself.

  • Logic gate lab for class

    Wire two buttons to an LED through gates you design. Students can test AND, OR and XOR by hand.

    Add: two push buttons, breadboard, jumper wires.

Builder ideas

  • Hello over the serial port

    Build a simple serial sender in Verilog. Watch your message appear in a terminal on your computer.

  • Seven segment counter

    Count button presses on a number display. It is a classic first step into counters and timers.

    Add: seven segment display add-on, push button.

  • Tone and melody maker

    Make square wave tones and play a short tune through a small speaker. Each button can play a different note.

    Add: small speaker, small amplifier board, push buttons.

  • Servo sweep for a puppet

    Make a precise pulse signal that swings a servo back and forth. Use it to nod a puppet head or wave a paper flag.

    Add: servo motor, jumper wires.

  • Frequency counter for science class

    Measure how fast a signal flips on and off. Test a buzzer, a fan sensor, or a signal generator.

    Add: jumper wires.

Expert ideas

  • Infrared message passer

    Use the built-in infrared window to send short messages between two sticks. It works like a tiny TV remote link.

  • Simple VGA terminal

    Draw text and color bars on an old monitor through a VGA add-on. It teaches how screens are drawn line by line.

    Add: VGA add-on, monitor.

  • Tiny soft processor

    Load a small RISC-V soft core and run short C programs on it. Memory is tight. Keep the programs small.

How open is it?

Openness level

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansSchematics only
  • Hardware licenseNot confirmed
  • Coding toolsFully open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

Lattice publishes a user manual with schematic diagrams, but no board layout files or open hardware license. The open toolchain is fully open, with Project IceStorm under the ISC license. The iCE40 chip itself is closed, and its file format was worked out by the IceStorm project.
