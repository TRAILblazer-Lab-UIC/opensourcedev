Luxonis OAK-D Lite

Luxonis

A small USB camera that sees depth and runs AI on board.

Overview

The OAK-D Lite is a small USB camera with three lenses and an AI chip inside. Two lenses work like a pair of eyes to measure depth. The third lens takes sharp color pictures. The camera does the heavy work itself and sends only the answers to your computer, which makes it a favorite for robots and people-counting projects.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Robots that need to see how far away things are
  • Object and people detection with distance attached
  • Python learners who want to try computer vision quickly
  • Pairing with a Raspberry Pi or laptop that lacks AI power

Not great for

  • Working on its own, as it needs a host computer
  • Very close objects, as depth works best from about 80 cm out
  • Tight budgets, as the price rose to about 269 USD in 2026
  • Fully open hardware, as its board files are unpublished

Specs in plain words

  • 1.4 TOPS of AI power lets the camera run neural networks itself and spare your computer the work.
  • The 13 megapixel color camera captures fine detail in each picture.
  • Two black and white cameras sit 7.5 cm apart, close to the gap between human eyes.
  • Depth works best from about 0.8 to 12 meters, roughly from arm's length to across a gym.
  • A built-in video encoder can record 4K at 30 frames per second.
  • One USB-C cable carries both power and data at about 2.5 to 3 watts.

ai_tops is the 1.4 TOPS AI figure from the Luxonis docs. The 4 TOPS headline is total compute. Size (91 x 28 x 17.5 mm, 61 g) is from CNX Software. The Luxonis docs page did not list dimensions. Mono cameras are OV7251 640 x 480 global shutter. Color camera is IMX214 13 MP rolling shutter. Power from Luxonis docs is 2.5 W to 3 W base plus up to 1 W AI, 0.5 W depth and 0.5 W encoder. CNX quoted 0.6 W standby and 4.5 W max at launch. Onboard RAM and flash sizes were not stated on fetched pages. The device has no user GPIO.

Things you could build

15 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • Measure distance to anything

    Run a depth example and point the camera around the room. Each spot on the screen shows how far away it is.

    Uses: Camera.

  • Spot objects with distance

    Run a ready-made object detection network on the camera. It labels a cup, a chair, or a person and says how far each one sits.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip.

  • Science class depth lab

    Let students measure the room with the depth view and compare it to a tape measure. Talk about how two eyes judge distance.

    Uses: Camera. Add: tape measure.

Builder ideas

  • Count visitors at an event

    Mount the camera above a doorway and count people walking past. Track how busy a library or a fair gets through the day.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip. Add: camera mount, laptop or small computer.

  • Garden critter watch

    Aim the camera at a bird feeder and detect animals as they arrive. Save a clip of each visitor with its distance.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip. Add: weatherproof box, laptop or small computer.

  • Hand wave light switch

    Detect a hand held up in front of the camera. Use it to turn a lamp on or skip a song with no touch.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip. Add: smart plug or relay board.

  • Exercise rep counter

    Run a body pose network while you do squats in front of the camera. Your computer counts each rep and cheers you on.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip.

  • Interactive art wall

    Project shapes on a wall that move as people walk closer. The depth camera tells the art how near each visitor stands.

    Uses: Camera. Add: projector, laptop.

  • Parking spot distance helper

    Mount the camera on a garage wall facing the car. A screen shows how many centimeters are left before the wall.

    Uses: Camera. Add: camera mount, small single-board computer, screen.

  • Tilt-aware camera rig

    Read the motion sensor to know which way the camera points. Level a time-lapse or correct a wobbly robot view.

    Uses: Sensors built in, Camera.

  • Low-bandwidth video recorder

    Let the camera squeeze 1080p video into H.265 on its own chip. A small computer saves long recordings without breaking a sweat.

    Uses: Camera. Add: small single-board computer, storage drive.

Expert ideas

  • Robot that avoids walls

    Put the camera on a small robot and read the depth map. The robot slows down and turns before it bumps into things.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip. Add: robot chassis, motor driver, small single-board computer, USB power bank.

  • Follow-me cart

    Teach a wheeled cart to track one person and keep a steady distance. It can carry tools around a garden.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip. Add: robot chassis, motor driver, small single-board computer, USB power bank.

  • 3D scan a small statue

    Turn an object slowly in front of the camera and collect depth frames. Combine them on your computer into a rough 3D shape.

    Uses: Camera. Add: turntable.

  • Train your own detector

    Train a small network to spot a thing you care about, such as ripe tomatoes. Convert it and run it on the camera.

    Uses: Camera, AI chip.

How open is it?

Openness level

Open CodeOpen BoardWild Open

Level 1 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansNot published
  • Hardware licenseNot confirmed
  • Coding toolsMostly open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

The DepthAI library is open source under the MIT license in Python and C++. The firmware inside the Myriad X chip and its native tools stay closed. Luxonis publishes only 3D models, enclosure files, and a datasheet for the OAK-D Lite board. An open DEV baseboard version has full design files under MIT, but no store sells it now.
