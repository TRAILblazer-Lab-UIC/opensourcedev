Overview

The MKL25Z128 is a small 32-bit Arm chip from NXP. Freescale made it first in 2012. Most people meet it on the FRDM-KL25Z board. The board has Arduino-style headers, a color light, and a touch slider. It was a popular board in university embedded systems classes. It suits students and tinkerers who want to learn C on real hardware.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning C and embedded basics in a class setting
  • Touch slider and color light experiments
  • Reading analog sensors with fine detail
  • Low-power gadgets that sleep most of the time

Not great for

  • Wireless projects, as it has no Wi-Fi or Bluetooth
  • Beginners who want Arduino or Python, as no official path exists
  • Following old mbed tutorials, as Arm shut down Mbed in July 2026
  • Motion projects on new boards, as recent boards ship without the motion sensor

Specs in plain words

  • The 48 MHz processor handles sensor reading and light control with room to spare.
  • 128 KB of storage holds a few thousand lines of C program code.
  • 16 KB of working memory is enough for a few hundred sensor readings at once.
  • A 16-bit analog input reads sensor voltages in very fine steps.
  • The chip can sleep on a tiny trickle of power and wake in a few millionths of a second.
  • Up to 66 pins give plenty of room for buttons, lights, and sensors.

Chip values come from the KL25 data sheet Rev 5 (August 2014) and the NXP FRDM-KL25Z page. GPIO is 66 for the MKL25Z128VLK4 in the 80-pin LQFP used on the FRDM-KL25Z. The ADC count of 16 is the number of distinct single-ended channel names in the data sheet pinout, including two differential pairs. Low power is the typical VLLS0 current at 25 C from the data sheet. The original board uses Mini-B USB connectors per the NXP kit note on cables. The usb_micro entry is the closest allowed value, and the new revision moves to USB-C. The MMA8451Q accelerometer is not soldered on board Rev I. As a result, the imu entry applies to earlier boards. The Zephyr device tree names an FXOS8700 instead. The board also has a capacitive touch slider.

Builder ideas

  • Touch slider dimmer

    Slide a finger along the touch strip to fade the color light up and down. It makes a neat desk lamp control.

    Uses: Sensors built in.

  • Color mood light

    Tap spots on the touch slider to pick red, green, or blue. Put the board in a frosted jar for a bedside glow.

    Uses: Sensors built in. Add: frosted jar.

  • Tilt maze game

    Tilt the board to steer a dot on a small screen. Older boards read the tilt with their built-in motion sensor.

    Uses: Sensors built in. Add: small screen.

  • Garden soil checker

    Read a soil moisture probe with the fine-grained analog input. The light turns red when the plants need water.

    Add: soil moisture sensor.

  • Room temperature logger

    Read a temperature sensor every minute and send the numbers to a laptop over USB. Students can graph a day in class.

    Add: temperature sensor.

  • Science light meter

    Measure light from bulbs or windows with a light sensor. Compare the readings for a science fair chart.

    Add: light sensor.

Expert ideas

  • Simple tone maker

    Use the built-in audio output pin to play notes on a small amplifier. Turn the touch slider into a pitch control.

    Uses: Sensors built in. Add: small amplifier, speaker.

  • USB keyboard reader

    Plug a USB keyboard into the board with an adapter. Show each typed letter on a small screen.

    Uses: Plugs in USB gadgets. Add: USB adapter cable, USB keyboard, small screen.

  • Battery weather station

    Read air sensors once a minute and sleep in between. The deep sleep mode helps a battery last for weeks.

    Uses: Sips power. Add: temperature and humidity sensor, battery pack.

  • Line-following robot car

    Drive two small motors from the Arduino-style headers. Two light sensors keep the car on a dark tape line.

    Add: robot car kit, motor driver, two light sensors.

  • Embedded course lab bench

    Work through timers, interrupts, and analog input labs in C. The board matches many university course guides.

    Add: jumper wires, breadboard.

How open is it?

Openness level

Level 1 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

The software tools are open. Zephyr, DAPLink, pyOCD, and Mbed OS use Apache-2.0, and the MCUXpresso SDK core uses BSD-3-Clause. NXP shares board schematics behind a free login, but no hardware license is stated. The Arm chip design stays closed.
