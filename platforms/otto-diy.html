Otto DIY

Otto DIY (Camilo Parra Palacio), now with HP Robots and Moravia Education

A small open-source walking robot you can 3D print and build at home.

Field marks: A 3D-printed biped robot with four SG90 servos and an HC-SR04 sensor up front, crossing the desk at the top speed of a cheap servo.

Overview

Otto is a small walking robot with an open design. You 3D print the body and add an Arduino Nano board, four small servo motors, a distance sensor, and a buzzer. It walks, dances, beeps, and avoids walls. Schools love it for teaching 3D printing, wiring, and coding in one project.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning to build a robot from scratch
  • Classes that already have a 3D printer
  • Coding with blocks or Arduino code
  • Remixing and designing your own robot body

Not great for

  • Quick plug-and-play fun with zero building
  • Wireless control on the classic build, which has no radio
  • Smart projects that need lots of memory or a camera

Specs in plain words

  • The Arduino Nano has 2 KB of working memory. That is enough for dance moves and sensor checks, but it fills up fast.
  • Four small servo motors move the hips and feet. They give Otto its funny waddle.
  • The sensor eyes use sound pulses to measure distance. They can tell a wall is close before Otto bumps it.
  • The robot is about 11 by 7 by 12 centimeters. It fits in the palm of a grown-up's hand.
  • Four AA batteries power the robot. A USB cable can power it while you code.

Specs describe the classic Otto DIY build with an Arduino Nano, from the Hackaday.io project and the OttoDIYLib README. The Nano uses a mini-USB connector, which is not in the connector vocabulary. Usb_micro is a stand-in. GPIO is the Nano's 14 digital plus 8 analog pins. The shield breaks them out as 3-pin servo-style headers. Size is the assembled robot, about 11 x 7 x 12 cm. The HP Otto version uses an ESP32 (dual-core Xtensa LX6, up to 240 MHz, 8 MB flash, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth 4.2), a 3.7 V 1800 mAh battery, USB-C, IR line sensors, 13 addressable RGB LEDs and continuous-rotation metal-gear servos, at 90 x 90 x 84 mm.

Starter ideas

  • Dance party robot

    Program Otto to sway, stomp, and wiggle to a beat. It becomes the star of any class party.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Hello wave greeter

    Otto sees a person walk up with its sensor eyes. It does a little bow and plays a happy tune.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Obstacle-avoiding walker

    The sensor eyes measure the distance ahead. Otto stops at a wall and turns to walk another way.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Story-telling actor

    Program moves for each part of a story. Otto acts out the scenes as students read aloud.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Cardboard Otto

    Build a body from the free papercraft files. No 3D printer is needed for this version.

    Add: cardboard, glue.

Builder ideas

  • Custom robot costume

    Design a new head or outfit in a free 3D design app. Print it and give your Otto a new look.

    Add: 3D printer, plastic filament.

  • Robot race day

    Each student tunes the walking steps of their own Otto. Line them up and see which one crosses the finish first.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Musical robot band

    Several Ottos each play a part of a song on their buzzers. They dance together as they play.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: extra Otto robots.

  • Plant guard

    Otto stands by a potted plant with a soil sensor plugged in. It beeps and waves once the soil gets dry.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: soil moisture sensor.

  • Light-up mood bot

    Add a small LED ring to the head. The lights change color with each dance move.

    Add: LED ring.

  • Robot with arms

    Print the humanoid remix with two extra servos for arms. Otto can now wave, point, and cheer.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: servo motor, 3D printer.

  • Walking gait lab

    Change the step size and speed in code. Measure how far Otto walks in ten seconds for each setting.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: measuring tape.

Expert ideas

  • Walk and roll ninja

    Build the OttoNinja remix with wheels in its feet. It can switch between walking and rolling.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: servo motor, 3D printer.

How open is it?

Openness level

Open CodeOpen BoardWild Open

Level 3 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansFull design files
  • Hardware licenseOpen license
  • Coding toolsFully open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design
  • OSHWACertified open hardware

Otto is fully open hardware with OSHWA certification CZ000001. The body files, code, and guides are free under CC BY-SA and GPL licenses. The chip on the Nano board is a closed design, and the newer HP Otto kit board has no published schematics.

Hardware license: CERN (OSHWA listing), CC BY-SA 4.0 (project README) · Software license: GPL-3.0 (OttoDIYLib, Otto Blockly), MIT (OttoNinja and OttoDIYPython repos)
