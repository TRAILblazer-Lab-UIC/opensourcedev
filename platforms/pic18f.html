PIC18F

Microchip Technology

Microchip's long-running 8-bit microcontroller family for embedded C projects.

Overview

PIC18F is Microchip's top line of 8-bit microcontrollers. It first came out in 2000 and still gets new parts. Newer chips such as the PIC18F47Q43 add small hardware helpers that run jobs without the main processor. It suits students in embedded C courses and builders of small, steady controllers.

How the scores work

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning embedded C the way many textbooks teach it
  • Small controllers for lights, fans, and motors
  • USB gadgets with older parts such as the PIC18F4550
  • Breadboard work with through-hole chip packages

Not great for

  • Beginners who want Arduino, MicroPython, or block coding
  • Wireless projects, with no radio on the chip
  • Open-source toolchains, with the main compiler closed
  • Moving code to other chips, with little carry-over between PIC families

Specs in plain words

  • A 64 MHz 8-bit core handles simple control jobs quickly.
  • 128 KB of flash holds a few thousand lines of C code.
  • 8 KB of working memory fits a small batch of sensor readings.
  • 1 KB of EEPROM keeps settings safe after the power goes off.
  • The 12-bit ADC reads dozens of analog inputs with fine detail.
  • Eight logic cells and six DMA channels move data and make small logic without the main code.

Chip values are for the PIC18F47Q43 (40 or 44 pins) from Microchip's PIC18-Q43 family table. The PIC18F57Q43 on the DM164150 Curiosity Nano has 44 I/O and 43 ADC channels. The 28-pin PIC18F27Q43 has 25 I/O and 24 channels. Older PIC18 parts differ a lot. The PIC18F4550 adds USB full speed but has less memory and a slower clock. Sleep current was not confirmed from a fetched source. Connectors and onboard parts describe the Curiosity Nano kits.

Things you could build

12 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Builder ideas

  • Blink the Curiosity LED

    Write a few lines of C in MPLAB X to flash the onboard LED. It is the classic first program for any chip.

  • Button doorbell chime

    Press the onboard switch to play a short tone on a buzzer. It teaches inputs, outputs, and timers.

    Add: small buzzer.

  • Night light with a sensor

    Read a light sensor with the 12-bit ADC and fade an LED on at dusk. Hang it in a hallway or a kid's room.

    Add: light sensor, LED.

  • Plant thirst alarm

    Check a soil moisture probe once a minute and beep for water. It suits a windowsill herb pot.

    Add: soil moisture sensor, small buzzer.

  • Fan speed controller

    Drive a small DC fan with PWM and set the speed with a knob. It keeps a 3D printer enclosure cool.

    Add: small DC fan, transistor board, potentiometer.

  • Mood light color mixer

    Mix red, green, and blue on an LED with three PWM channels. Fade through colors for a bedroom or an art piece.

    Add: RGB LED.

  • Serial temperature logger

    Read a temperature sensor and send values to a computer over a UART. Graph a day of classroom temperatures.

    Add: temperature sensor, USB serial adapter.

Expert ideas

  • Touch button lamp

    Use the ADC's built-in touch sensing to make a copper pad into a switch. Tap it to toggle a desk light.

    Add: copper tape, relay board.

  • USB macro keypad

    Use a PIC18F4550 board to act as a USB keyboard. Each button types a shortcut for games or video editing.

    Add: PIC18F4550 prototype board, push buttons.

  • Digital logic lab

    Build small logic gates inside the configurable logic cells. Students can compare them with breadboard chips.

    Add: push buttons, LEDs.

  • Stepper motor turntable

    Turn a small stepper motor one step at a time for a photo turntable. It helps make 3D scans of small objects.

    Add: stepper motor, stepper driver board.

  • Simple synth tones

    Use the numerically controlled oscillators to make clean musical notes. Play a tune through a speaker with buttons.

    Add: small speaker, small amplifier board, push buttons.

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

The PIC18 chip design is closed. MPLAB X and the XC8 compiler are free downloads but closed source. The NightUSB dev board is open hardware under CERN-OHL-S-2.0 and is OSHWA certified.
