Overview

Pirate Audio is a range of small add-on boards for the Raspberry Pi. Each board has a little square screen, four buttons, and one kind of sound gear. Choices include a line out, a headphone amp, a stereo speaker amp, a built-in speaker, or two microphones. It suits music lovers and tinkerers who want a tidy music player with no coding.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Building a small music player with album art
  • Turning a Pi into a Spotify, AirPlay, or internet radio receiver
  • Recording voice clips with the Dual Mic board
  • Simple Python projects that mix sound, a screen, and buttons

Not great for

  • Working alone. Each board has no brain and needs a Raspberry Pi.
  • Recording and playing sound on one board. The Dual Mic board has no audio output.
  • Easy buying everywhere. Some boards are out of stock or discontinued at several stores.
  • Big room sound. The amps are small and suit a desk or kitchen.

Specs in plain words

  • The 1.3 inch screen is about the size of a postage stamp. It is sharp enough for album art and short menus.
  • The PCM5100A sound chip on the Line-out and Headphone Amp boards plays high quality stereo music.
  • The 3W Stereo Amp drives two small speakers loud enough to fill a desk or kitchen.
  • The Speaker board has its own 1 watt speaker built in. It works out of the box for quiet listening.
  • The Dual Mic board has two tiny microphones for recording voice and room sound.
  • Each board is about 65 by 30 millimeters. That is about the size of a Raspberry Pi Zero.

Pirate Audio is an add-on board with no processor of its own. As a result, CPU and memory fields are null. The speaker is only on the Speaker board, and the microphones are only on the Dual Mic board. The audio jack is on the Line-out and Headphone Amp boards. By contrast, the amp boards use push-fit speaker terminals. Pinout.xyz lists 14 GPIO used by the Speaker board, including SPI for the LCD, I2S and an amp enable pin on BCM 25. The DAC boards use the hifiberry-dac overlay, and the Dual Mic uses the adau7002-simple overlay.

Level 1 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

The setup software is open and shared under the MIT license. It builds on open tools such as Linux drivers, Python and Mopidy. Pimoroni does not publish schematics or board files, and the chips and the Pi boot firmware stay closed.
