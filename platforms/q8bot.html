Q8bot

Yufeng (Eric) Wu, with the UCLA Robotics and Mechanisms Lab (RoMeLa)

A palm-sized open-source walking robot with four legs and no wires.

Overview

Q8bot is a small four-legged robot about the size and weight of a smartphone. Eight smart servos move four legs, and every part plugs straight into one circuit board. It walks, trots, bounds, and jumps. It suits robotics students, researchers, and makers ready for a weekend build.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning how legged robots walk
  • Robotics classes and swarm experiments
  • Makers who enjoy a hands-on build with a 3D printer
  • Studying a fully published robot design

Not great for

  • Tight budgets, as parts cost about 300 to 400 US dollars
  • Buying ready to run, as no store sells the original kit
  • Complete beginners, as it needs soldering and careful assembly
  • Walking alone today, as the gait code runs on a laptop

Specs in plain words

  • About 220 grams is close to the weight of a smartphone.
  • Eight smart servos give each leg two joints of precise motion.
  • A top trot of 0.43 meters per second is about five body lengths every second.
  • Two rechargeable 14500 cells hold about 2000 mAh, which keeps it walking for a good play session.
  • An ESP32-C3 board on the robot gets joint angles by radio from a second board in the laptop.

Size80 x 70 x 70 mm

Size is about 8 cm long, 7 cm wide and 7 cm tall standing, folding to 12 x 7 x 5 cm, from the arXiv paper. Weight is about 220 g. The XIAO ESP32-C3 values (single RISC-V core at 160 MHz, 400 KB SRAM, 4 MB flash) come from the CNX Software article on the Q8botOne successor, which uses the same chip family. The servos run on a TTL serial bus from the ESP32-C3. The PCB has an I2C expansion port and a MAX17043 battery fuel gauge footprint. Gait generation and kinematics run on the laptop. It is not on the robot.

Things you could build

Builder ideas

  • First walk across the table

    Flash the ready-made firmware and run the laptop gait code. Watch the robot trot across a desk.

    Uses: Wi-Fi.

  • Keyboard driving game

    Steer the robot with arrow keys on the laptop. Race friends around a taped track on the floor.

    Uses: Wi-Fi. Add: masking tape.

  • Gamepad remote control

    Read a USB gamepad on the laptop and turn stick moves into walking commands. It feels like driving a toy.

    Uses: Wi-Fi. Add: USB gamepad.

  • Robot dance routine

    Write a sequence of poses and steps in Python. Sync the moves to a song for a school show.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Gait science lab

    Compare a walk, a trot, and a bound on the same track. Time each run and chart speed against battery level.

    Uses: Drives motors, Battery ready. Add: stopwatch.

  • Leg kinematics lesson

    Use the five-bar legs to show how joint angles turn into foot paths. Students change angles and see the foot move.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Custom printed shells

    Design a new body cover in CAD and print it. Turn the robot into a mascot for your club.

    Add: 3D printer filament.

Expert ideas

  • Jump height contest

    Tune the jump gait and measure how high the robot leaves the ground. Film it in slow motion for a class.

    Uses: Drives motors.

  • Balance with a motion sensor

    Plug a motion sensor into the I2C port and read body tilt. Adjust the legs to stay level on a ramp.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: motion sensor board.

  • Obstacle course explorer

    Add a small distance sensor and stop or turn before a wall. Set up a box maze for the robot.

    Uses: Drives motors. Add: distance sensor board, cardboard boxes.

  • Tiny robot swarm

    Build two or three robots and send each one commands from a single laptop. Try follow-the-leader walks.

    Uses: Wi-Fi, Drives motors. Add: extra servos, extra printed parts.

  • Onboard walking brain

    Move the gait code from the laptop onto the robot's ESP32-C3. The robot then walks with no laptop nearby.

    Uses: Drives motors, Battery ready.

How open is it?

Openness level

Open CodeOpen BoardWild Open

Level 3 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansFull design files
  • Hardware licenseOpen license
  • Coding toolsFully open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

Everything is published under the MIT license, including the PCB sources, Gerbers, STEP files, SolidWorks CAD, firmware, and Python control code. The PCB was drawn in Altium, a closed tool. Gerbers and a schematic PDF are included too. The servo firmware, the ESP32-C3 radio stack, and the chips themselves stay closed.
