RP2350

Raspberry Pi Ltd

A tiny, low-cost brain chip from Raspberry Pi for blinking, sensing, and building gadgets.

Field marks: The RP2350 ships with two Arm cores and two RISC-V cores, lets software pick which pair runs, and still costs five dollars on a Pico 2.

Overview

The RP2350 is the second microcontroller chip made by Raspberry Pi. It powers the five dollar Raspberry Pi Pico 2 and many other small boards. It suits first-time tinkerers, teachers, and seasoned makers alike. It stands out for its two kinds of processor cores and its special PIO helpers for custom signals.

How the scores work

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning to code hardware with MicroPython or CircuitPython
  • Sensor projects, lights, buttons, and small robots
  • USB gadgets such as keypads and music controllers
  • Retro computers and video output on an HDMI screen
  • Low-cost class sets for schools

Not great for

  • Wireless projects on the plain chip, which has no radio built in
  • Camera or voice AI work with its small memory
  • Running a full desktop or Linux programs

Specs in plain words

  • Two cores at 150 MHz let it read sensors and run a light show at the same time.
  • 520 KB of working memory holds a few thousand sensor readings at once.
  • 4 MB of storage on the Pico 2 fits your code plus small data files.
  • 12 PIO state machines act like tiny helpers that speak unusual digital signals by themselves.
  • The Pico 2 has 30 pins for wires, with 4 that can read changing voltages from sensors.
  • In its deepest rest it sips about 57 microamps, which helps battery projects last.

Values are for the RP2350A on the Raspberry Pi Pico 2, which has 4 MB flash, no PSRAM, a micro-USB port and a BOOTSEL button. The RP2350B has 48 GPIO and 8 ADC inputs. Boards such as the Pimoroni Pico Plus 2 and SparkFun Pro Micro RP2350 add 16 MB flash and 8 MB PSRAM. The Pico 2 W adds Wi-Fi and Bluetooth 5.2 through a CYW43439 radio. low_power_ua is the sum of supply currents in the P1.7 low-power state from datasheet table 1692 (about 188 uW at 3.3 V). I2S, HSTX video and other buses are made with PIO or HSTX in place of fixed peripherals.

Things you could build

14 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • Blinking traffic light model

    Wire three colored lights and make them change in order. It is a gentle first project for any classroom.

    Add: red yellow and green LEDs, resistors, breadboard.

  • Houseplant thirst alarm

    Read a soil sensor and flash a light once the pot gets dry. Your plants will tattle on you before they wilt.

    Add: soil moisture sensor, LED.

  • Reaction time game

    Light an LED at a random moment and time how fast a friend presses a button. Keep score on the computer screen.

    Add: push button, LED, breadboard.

  • Classroom weather logger

    Record temperature and humidity every few minutes. Students can graph a week of readings in a spreadsheet.

    Add: temperature and humidity sensor.

  • Glowing LED strip art

    Drive a strip of color LEDs with smooth patterns. Hang it behind a painting or along a shelf.

    Add: LED strip, 5 volt power supply.

  • Light theremin

    Turn hand height over a light sensor into changing tones on a tiny speaker. Wave your hands and play spooky music.

    Add: light sensor, piezo buzzer.

Builder ideas

  • Custom shortcut keypad

    Build a small pad of buttons that acts like a keyboard. Each key can paste a phrase or launch a shortcut.

    Add: push buttons, small enclosure.

  • Simple MIDI drum pad

    Turn buttons or pressure pads into a USB music controller. Music apps on a computer see it as an instrument.

    Add: push buttons, pressure sensors.

  • Small two-wheel robot

    Steer a little robot car with two motors and a distance sensor. It can wander a room and turn away from walls.

    Add: motor driver board, two small motors with wheels, distance sensor, battery pack.

  • Battery-powered bird box counter

    Count visits to a nest box with a light beam sensor. The chip sleeps between visits to save the batteries.

    Uses: Sips power. Add: break beam sensor, battery pack.

  • Heart rate demo for health class

    Read a fingertip pulse sensor and blink in time with each heartbeat. Students can compare resting and active rates.

    Add: pulse sensor.

Expert ideas

  • Retro game console on a TV

    Use the fast video output to draw pixel graphics on an HDMI screen. Pair it with a USB gamepad for a tiny arcade.

    Uses: Plugs in USB gadgets. Add: HDMI adapter board, USB gamepad, HDMI screen.

  • USB keyboard to old computer adapter

    Plug a modern USB keyboard into the board and translate keypresses for a vintage machine. PIO handles odd old signals.

    Uses: Plugs in USB gadgets. Add: USB socket adapter, level shifter.

  • Open RISC-V teaching lab

    Switch the chip to its open RISC-V cores and compare them with the Arm cores. It makes chip design feel less mysterious.

Level 3 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

Raspberry Pi shares a minimal RP2350 board design in KiCad under the MIT license. The Pico SDK is open source under BSD-3-Clause and builds with free tools. The RISC-V cores are open designs under Apache-2.0, but the Arm cores and the rest of the chip stay closed.
