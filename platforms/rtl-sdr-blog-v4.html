Overview

The RTL-SDR Blog V4 is a small USB radio receiver. Free apps on your computer turn it into a scanner, a plane tracker or a shortwave radio. It only listens and never transmits. It suits curious beginners, students, and radio hobbyists on a small budget.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Learning radio on a small budget
  • Tracking planes and catching weather satellites
  • Shortwave listening with no extra converter
  • Science projects such as meteor counting and radio astronomy

Not great for

  • Sending any signal, as it is receive-only
  • Signals above 1.766 GHz such as Wi-Fi
  • Buyers who want a new unit, as the V4 is discontinued and the V4L replaced it
  • People who want an open hardware design

Specs in plain words

  • It hears from 500 kHz to 1.766 GHz. That spans AM radio up past the plane and weather bands.
  • It shows about 2.5 MHz of radio at once. That fits a handful of FM stations on screen together.
  • The 1 PPM TCXO is a steady clock. Stations stay put and do not drift as it warms up.
  • The bias tee sends a little power up the antenna cable. It can run a small signal amplifier.
  • The metal case pulls heat away and helps block noise.
  • Notch filters cut strong FM and TV signals. Weaker stations nearby come through more clearly.

Specs from the V4 datasheet V1.0. Tuner R828D, ADC RTL2832U 8-bit, 500 kHz to 1.766 GHz, 2.56 MHz stable bandwidth (up to 3.2 MHz with drops), 50 ohm input, 1x SMA, USB-A male, 1 PPM TCXO. No transmit capability. radios lists sub_ghz only because the vocab has no general SDR value. Coverage spans HF, VHF and UHF to 1.766 GHz. The SMA antenna input is not in the connector vocab. A USB-C variant (V4c) appears on rtl-sdr.com, but its details were not read. The bias tee LED is the only onboard indicator. Size and weight were not found.

Things you could build

14 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • Computer FM radio

    Tune local FM stations in SDR++ and see them as bright stripes on the screen. It is the classic first test.

    Add: dipole antenna kit.

  • Home plane tracker

    Run a free ADS-B app to put nearby aircraft on a live map. Leave it running to see daily flight paths over your home.

    Add: dipole antenna kit.

  • Shortwave listening from home

    Tune shortwave stations from around the world with no extra converter. A long wire outdoors helps a lot.

    Add: long wire antenna.

  • Ham radio listening post

    Listen to local amateur radio chatter and repeaters. It is a friendly way to learn before taking a license exam.

  • Classroom spectrum tour

    Project the waterfall display in class. Students see FM, phones, and other signals as shapes and colors.

    Add: projector or big screen.

Builder ideas

  • Weather satellite pictures

    Catch passing weather satellites with a simple outdoor antenna. Free software turns the signal into a cloud photo.

    Add: satellite antenna.

  • Ship tracker by the coast

    Decode ship position signals near a harbor or lake. Watch boats move on a map from your desk.

    Add: outdoor antenna.

  • Your own weather station feed

    Pick up the signal from your own wireless outdoor thermometer with open decoder software. Log temperature to a spreadsheet.

    Add: wireless weather sensor.

  • Meteor echo counter

    Listen for faint pings as meteors streak through the sky. Count them during a meteor shower for a science project.

    Add: outdoor antenna.

  • Raspberry Pi listening station

    Plug the dongle into a small computer and run it day and night. Share your plane or weather feed with friends.

    Add: single-board computer, power supply, outdoor antenna.

  • Check your own mesh nodes

    Watch the licence-free LoRa band while your own Meshtastic nodes send. See each burst and how strong it lands.

  • Antenna building challenge

    Build a simple dipole from wire or tape measures. Compare it with the kit antenna on the same station.

    Add: wire, coax cable.

Expert ideas

  • Hydrogen line radio telescope

    Point a small dish or horn at the Milky Way and detect hydrogen gas at 1420 MHz. The bias tee powers the needed amplifier.

    Add: small dish or horn antenna, low-noise amplifier, filter.

  • GNU Radio signal lab

    Build your own receiver as a flow chart in GNU Radio. Add filters and decoders block by block.
