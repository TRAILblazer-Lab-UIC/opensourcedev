Overview

ThunderScope is a four-channel oscilloscope that turns your computer into a bench scope. The box sends every sample over Thunderbolt or USB4, and a PCIe card version plugs into a desktop. Your computer handles triggering, storage, and display. It suits engineers, serious hobbyists, and people who want to build their own test tools.

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Seeing fast signals on four channels at once
  • Recording very long captures into your computer's memory
  • Writing custom triggers and signal processing
  • Labs that want a scope with fully open design files

Not great for

  • Absolute beginners on a small budget
  • Laptops without a Thunderbolt or USB4 port
  • Quick setup on macOS, which needs security settings changed for now
  • Quick delivery, with shipping status still unclear

Specs in plain words

  • 500 MHz of bandwidth means it can show signals that switch hundreds of millions of times per second.
  • 1 GS/s means it takes one billion snapshots of the signal every second.
  • Four channels let you compare four wires side by side.
  • Memory depth comes from your computer's RAM. More RAM means longer recordings.
  • It draws under 15 watts from the cable or slot. You need no separate power brick.
  • The box is about the size of a hardback book at 145 by 103 by 31 mm.

adc_channels is the four scope channels. Sample rate is 1 GS/s at 8 bits and 660 MS/s at 12 bits per the docs. CNX Software reported 500 MS/s for 12-bit at launch. Memory depth is set by host RAM. CNX reported a software limit of 1 Gpts per channel. The host link is PCIe Gen 2 x4, either direct (TS-PCIe card) or tunnelled over Thunderbolt/USB4 (TS-USB4 box, USB-C port, 40 Gbps max). swd_jtag reflects the FPGA JTAG debug header. Inputs are BNC, which has no vocabulary entry. Size is the TS-USB4 enclosure (360 g). The PCIe card is 132 x 120 x 20 mm and 150 g, per CNX Software. The gateware README lists a "prod" Rev5 A35T target and a "dev" Rev5 A50T target. CNX named the XC7A35T-2CSG325C.

Things you could build

11 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • See your first signal

    Clip a probe onto a blinking LED circuit and watch the square wave appear on your screen. It turns invisible electricity into a picture.

    Add: scope probe, simple LED circuit.

  • Classroom sound wave lab

    Feed a tone from a small amplifier into the scope and project the waveform for the class. Students can see pitch and volume change the wave shape.

    Add: scope probe, small speaker amplifier.

  • Check a power supply for noise

    Probe the output of a phone charger or bench supply. You can spot ripple and spikes that might upset a project.

    Add: scope probe.

Builder ideas

  • Debug a sensor bus

    Watch the clock and data lines of a sensor talking to a microcontroller. The software can decode the messages into readable bytes.

    Add: two scope probes, microcontroller board, sensor module.

  • Tune a synth module

    Watch the output of a homemade synthesizer oscillator on all four channels at once. It helps you shape cleaner tones.

    Add: scope probes, synth kit.

  • Measure a motor driver

    Look at the pulse signals that set a motor's speed. You can compare the command signal to the current flowing in the coil.

    Add: scope probes, motor driver board, small DC motor.

  • Science fair signal recorder

    Record long stretches of a slow signal into your computer's memory. Review the whole run later and share screenshots.

    Add: scope probe.

  • Makerspace shared bench scope

    Set up a PCIe card in a shared lab computer. Members get a four-channel scope on a big monitor at the workbench.

    Add: desktop computer with a free PCIe slot, scope probes.

Expert ideas

  • Write a custom trigger

    Add your own trigger logic to the host software. The scope can catch a rare glitch that normal triggers miss.

  • Software radio experiments

    Stream raw samples from a small antenna into your own signal processing code. You can study radio signals in the low hundreds of megahertz.

    Add: small antenna, coax adapter.

  • Modify the FPGA gateware

    Change the open LiteX gateware to filter or tag samples on the board. It is a real lesson in test-tool design.

How open is it?

Openness level

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansFull design files
  • Hardware licenseOpen license
  • Coding toolsMostly open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

The KiCad schematics and board files are open under CERN-OHL-P-2.0, and OSHWA certified it as CA000017. The host software is MIT and the LiteX gateware is BSD-2-Clause. Building the gateware needs AMD Vivado, a free but closed tool. The FPGA and front-end chips are closed.
