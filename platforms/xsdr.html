Overview

The xSDR is a software defined radio from Wavelet Lab on a small M.2 card. A software defined radio turns radio waves into data your computer can study and change. This card has two receive and two transmit channels, and it covers 30 MHz to 3.8 GHz. It suits radio researchers, ham operators, and engineers who want a lot of radio in a very small space.

How the scores work

Good fit or not?

Good for

  • Spectrum monitoring and radio experiments from 30 MHz to 3.8 GHz
  • Cellular network research with srsRAN
  • Two-antenna projects such as direction finding
  • Fitting a capable radio inside a laptop or small computer

Not great for

  • Beginners, as it costs about 549 US dollars and assumes radio skills
  • Shortwave listening, as it starts at 30 MHz
  • Quick setup, as the tiny antenna sockets need adapters and pigtail cables
  • Fast data over a USB-only M.2 slot, which limits throughput

Specs in plain words

  • 30 MHz to 3.8 GHz covers FM radio, aircraft, weather satellites, phone bands, and 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi.
  • 90 MHz of bandwidth lets you watch a slice of the airwaves wider than the whole FM band at once.
  • Two receive and two transmit channels let it work with two antennas at the same time.
  • The Artix-7 FPGA is a rewirable chip that crunches radio samples before they reach your computer.
  • The card is 30 by 22 millimeters, about the size of a postage stamp.
  • It draws about 1.9 watts, a little more than a phone charger trickle.

No CPU. The FPGA and LMS7002M do the work, and the host runs the software. Docs list XC7A50T and XC7A35T variants. CNX says prototypes used the pin-compatible XC7A35T. Sample rate is 100 MSPS in the docs and 122.88 MSPS SISO, 80 MSPS MIMO per CNX. Clock reference is a 0.5 ppm 26 MHz oscillator. GPIO is the 12+2-pin 1.8 V header. Onboard TMP114 temperature sensor per CNX. No radio vocabulary value fits a general SDR. As a result, radios is empty.

Things you could build

13 ideas sized to what this board can do. Starter ideas suit first-time builders.

Starter ideas

  • Tune in local FM radio

    Open SDR++ on your computer and scan the FM band. Watch each station appear as a bright stripe on the waterfall screen.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, small antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

Builder ideas

  • Track planes overhead

    Decode the position signals that airliners send at 1090 MHz. Plot the planes on a live map of your town.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, 1090 MHz antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

  • Pictures from weather satellites

    Catch passing weather satellites near 137 MHz. Turn their signals into cloud photos for a science class.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, V-shaped satellite antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

  • Map the radio noise at school

    Sweep from 30 MHz to 3.8 GHz and log how busy each band is. Students can compare the gym, the library, and the parking lot.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, wideband antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

  • Read home weather sensors

    Pick up the 433 MHz signals from cheap outdoor thermometers. Log the readings to a chart on your computer.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, 433 MHz antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

  • Follow a weather balloon

    Track radiosondes near 400 MHz as they rise from a weather station. Find where they land and go look for one.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, 400 MHz antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

  • Learn radio with GNU Radio

    Build flowgraphs to filter, mix, and decode signals step by step. It is a hands-on lab for a radio or physics course.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, small antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

  • Radio in a web browser

    Try the wsdr.io browser platform with the card on USB. Share a simple receiver page with a club or class.

    Add: M.2 to USB-C adapter, small antenna, antenna pigtail cable.

Expert ideas

  • Listen to the hydrogen line

    Point a small dish at the Milky Way and look for the faint 1420 MHz glow of hydrogen gas. Plot how it shifts across the sky.

    Add: small dish antenna, low noise amplifier, M.2 to PCIe breakout board.

  • Find a signal's direction

    Use the two receive channels together to compare when a signal reaches each antenna. Point an arrow toward a lost tracker or a hidden beacon.

    Add: M.2 to PCIe breakout board, two matched antennas, antenna cables.

  • A lab cellular network

    Run srsRAN and test phones on a private, shielded bench network. Research groups use this to study how mobile networks work.

    Add: M.2 to PCIe breakout board, antennas, shielded test box, test SIM cards.

  • Ham radio transceiver

    Licensed hams can send and receive on the 2 meter and 70 centimeter bands. Build your own digital voice or data modes in software.

    Add: M.2 to PCIe breakout board, ham band antenna, power amplifier.

  • Stacked radio array

    Link several boards to one shared clock for phase-matched channels. Steer a beam of reception in software for advanced research.

    Add: extra xSDR boards, clock distribution board, M.2 to PCIe breakout boards, antennas.

How open is it?

Openness level

Open CodeOpen BoardWild Open

Level 2 of 3. Wild Open shares the most.

  • Board plansSchematics only
  • Hardware licenseNot confirmed
  • Coding toolsMostly open
  • The chip itselfClosed chip design

The host software in usdr-lib is open under the MIT license. Wavelet Lab lists the FPGA gateware under CERN-OHL-P-2.0, but the xSDR gateware source was not found yet. Only a schematic PDF is public for the board, and the hardware repo has no license. Rebuilding the gateware needs the closed AMD Vivado tool. The radio chip is closed too.
